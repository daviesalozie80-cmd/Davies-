## PART 7: How to recognise an interesting story

### The 8 levels of information, using one subject: Portugal's housing

| Type | Definition | Portugal example | Video value |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary** | True, but expected | "House prices in Portugal rose." | Low. It's a news headline. |
| **Interesting** | True, with a link the viewer hadn't made | "Prices rose fastest in Lisbon, where tourism is concentrated." | Medium |
| **Surprising** | Contradicts what people assume | "Portugal has more homes than households, and yet locals can't find one to rent." | **High:** it's a paradox |
| **Controversial** | Reasonable people disagree | "Did golden visas and tax breaks for foreigners price out locals?" | High, but needs balance |
| **Historical discovery** | A past cause that explains the present | "The 2011 bailout pushed Portugal to attract foreign money. The housing boom began there." | High: an "aha" origin |
| **Unexpected consequence** | Policy A led to effect B nobody intended | "A scheme to rescue the economy ended up pushing out the young workers the economy needs." | **Very high** |
| **Unanswered question** | Open and debated | "Can Portugal stop the brain drain without crashing house prices?" | High: a strong ending |
| **Important development** | A new change with big effects | "The government ended golden visas for property in 2023." | A news peg for timing |

### Turning ordinary information into a story: the 5-step upgrade
1. **Start with an ordinary fact:** "House prices in Portugal rose."
2. **Find the paradox:** what makes it strange? "...despite empty homes and low wages."
3. **Find the cause chain:** bailout → hunger for foreign money → golden visas and tax breaks + tourism boom → short-term lets → rents outrun wages.
4. **Find the human:** a 30-year-old Lisbon nurse who earns about the national average and lives with her parents. *(Use real data on wages and rents. The character is illustrative, so label her that way.)*
5. **Find the stakes and open question:** young Portuguese emigrating, the population ageing, and "who will pay for this in 20 years?"

**Result title:** *"Portugal Sold Itself to Save Itself"* or *"Why Living in Portugal Became Impossible"*.

1. **"The country that has more homes than people, and still a housing crisis"** (e.g. empty homes in Japan, Spain or Portugal). *Why watch:* a paradox.

## PART 8: How to think like an expert

### The 10 questions experts always ask
1. **Compared to what?** A 3% inflation rate is high for Switzerland and low for Turkey. Always compare with the past, with peers, and with targets.
2. **Per person? Real? Over what period?** (Module 1's expert habit.)
3. **Who pays and who benefits?** Every policy has winners and losers.
4. **What's the mechanism?** Not just "A happened then B", but *how* A causes B.
5. **What else changed at the same time?** Correlation isn't causation. (Correlation = two things moving together; causation = one actually causing the other.)
6. **Is this structural or cyclical?**
7. **Where's the money coming from, and where does it go?** (Follow the flows: trade, capital, remittances.)
8. **What currency is the debt in, and who holds it?**
9. **What do the incentives push people to do?** (Incentive = a reward or penalty that shapes behaviour.)
10. **What would change my mind?** Experts name the evidence that would prove them wrong.

### What beginners overlook
- **Base effects:** "Prices fell 5%!" may only mean last year's spike is fading.
- **Exchange-rate illusions:** a country's GDP in dollars can "fall" just because its currency weakened.
- **Revisions:** first GDP estimates often get revised.
- **Averages hiding inequality:** average wealth can rise while most people get poorer.
- **Time lags:** interest-rate changes take about 1–2 years to fully hit the economy.
- **Survivorship bias:** studying only successful countries (or channels!) and copying traits that the failures also had.

### Common misconceptions (and the correction)

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| "Printing money always causes hyperinflation." | It *can* (Zimbabwe, Weimar), but huge QE after 2008 didn't cause high consumer inflation. Context matters: demand, supply, trust and expectations. |
| "Government debt must be paid off like a credit card." | Governments with their own currency usually roll debt over. Risk depends on interest vs growth, currency and creditors. |
| "A trade deficit means a country is losing." | It often reflects strong demand or capital inflows. The US has run deficits for decades while remaining the largest economy. |
| "Tariffs are paid by the foreign country." | Studies of the 2018–19 US tariffs found most costs fell on US importers and consumers. |
| "GDP measures well-being." | It measures output, not happiness, inequality or sustainability. |
| "Immigration always lowers wages" / "never affects wages." | Research generally finds small average effects, which vary by sector and period. Avoid both extremes. |
| "The dollar is about to collapse." | Its share of reserves has drifted down slowly, but no replacement has the same depth or trust. Treat collapse claims as speculation. |

### Fact vs assumption vs opinion vs prediction
- **Fact:** checkable. "Japan's debt is about 250% of GDP (IMF, 2024)."
- **Assumption:** taken for granted without proof. "Investors will always trust Japan."
- **Opinion/interpretation:** a judgement. "Japan's debt is dangerous."
- **Prediction:** about the future. "Japan will face a crisis by 2030."

**Script language for each:**
- Fact: "According to the IMF…"
- Opinion: "Many economists argue…" / "Critics say…"
- Prediction: "If current trends continue…" / "One scenario is…"

### An expert analysis routine (use it on any situation)
1. **Describe:** what exactly happened? (Numbers, dates, sources.)
2. **Decompose:** what are the 3–5 forces involved? (Supply, demand, policy, external shock, politics.)
3. **Mechanise:** draw arrows showing how each force causes the result.
4. **Compare:** find a similar country or period. What was different?
5. **Weigh:** which force is biggest? What do credible sources say?
6. **Stakeholders:** who wins and who loses?
7. **Scenarios:** best case, base case, worst case, and what would trigger each.
8. **Uncertainty:** what don't we know?

## PART 9: Generating your own topics

### 10 idea sources and how to mine each

| Source | Where to look | Prompt to ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Historical events | Wikipedia "List of economic crises", IMF history pages | "What did this event cause that still matters today?" |
| Real-world problems | Cost-of-living news, Reddit (r/AskEconomics, country subreddits) | "Why does this happen *here* and not elsewhere?" |
| Unusual discoveries | Resource finds, unexpected data releases | "Who gains and who loses from this discovery?" |
| Important people | Central bankers, reformers, dictators, founders | "What one decision did they make that changed the country?" |
| Interesting statistics | Our World in Data, IMF DataMapper, OECD | "Which country is the outlier on this chart, and why?" |
| Unanswered questions | Economist debates, academic blogs | "What do experts disagree about?" |
| Unexpected outcomes | Policy evaluations | "What did this policy do that nobody intended?" |
| Public reports | The IMF *World Economic Outlook* (April and October), central bank reports, OECD *Economic Surveys* | "What's the scariest or most surprising chart in this report?" |
| New developments | Elections, budgets, rate decisions, sanctions | "What's the structural story behind this news?" |
| Common misunderstandings | Comments on popular videos, viral posts | "What does everyone get wrong about this?" |

### One subject → 10 original ideas (worked example: **Japan**)
1. **Rise:** How Japan went from war ruins to the world's #2 economy.
2. **Fall:** The bubble so big the Imperial Palace grounds were said to be worth more than California.
3. **Mystery:** How Japan owes 250% of GDP and hasn't collapsed.
4. **People:** Why Japan has millions of empty houses (*akiya*).
5. **Demographics:** Who will pay Japan's pensions?
6. **Comparison:** Japan vs South Korea: whose demographic crisis is worse?
7. **Misconception:** "Japan's lost decades" — were they really lost? (Some economists argue that, measured per working-age person, Japan's growth was close to other rich countries' and better than the "lost decades" label suggests.) [debated]
8. **Policy:** What happened when Japan ended negative interest rates?
9. **Global link:** How Japanese savings fund the world (the "carry trade").
10. **Future:** Can robots and immigration save Japan's economy? (Present as scenarios.)

**The 10 "lenses" behind this list are your daily idea generator:**
- rise
- fall
- mystery
- people
- demographics
- comparison
- misconception
- policy
- global link
- future

### The daily topic formula
> **[Country or system] + [Lens] + [Paradox or stake] = Topic**
>
> - Canada + Comparison + "rich country, poor productivity" → *"Why Canada Is Turning Into the Next UK"*
> - Saudi Arabia + Policy + "oil-rich yet building a desert megacity" → *"Why Saudi's Megaprojects Keep Shrinking"*
> - The world + Mystery + "everyone is in debt" → *"If Every Country Is in Debt… Who's the Lender?"*

### The 15-minute daily routine
1. **5 min:** skim one data source (e.g. an Our World in Data chart, or one IMF or OECD headline).
2. **5 min:** write 3 ideas using the formula above.
3. **5 min:** score each idea 1–5 on:
   - personal stakes
   - paradox or surprise
   - mappability
   - evidence availability
   - news timing

   Keep anything scoring 18 or more out of 25 in an "idea bank" spreadsheet.
