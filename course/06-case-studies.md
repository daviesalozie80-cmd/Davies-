### 1. Who owns America's debt?
1. **What it is:** the US federal government's accumulated borrowing. It passed **$36 trillion in gross terms during 2024**. (Check the current figure at fiscaldata.treasury.gov.)
2. **How it works:** the Treasury sells bonds (Treasuries). Buyers include:
   - **Other parts of the US government itself**: "intragovernmental" holdings such as the Social Security trust funds, roughly a fifth of the total.
   - **The Federal Reserve**, which bought heavily during QE.
   - **US investors**: mutual funds, banks, pension funds, insurers, individuals.
   - **Foreign holders**, roughly a quarter to a third of publicly held debt. **Japan** has been the largest foreign holder; China and the UK are among the next largest. (Check the latest Treasury *TIC* data.)
3. **Why it matters:** Treasuries are the world's "safe asset". Their yields set borrowing costs globally.
4. **What makes it interesting:** most of the debt is owed to **Americans and US institutions**, not to China, which reverses a popular belief. And the government owes a big chunk *to itself*.
5. **What viewers should understand:** debt's danger depends on interest costs vs growth, and on continued demand for Treasuries, not on the headline number alone.
6. **Story:** *"America Owes $36 Trillion. Here's Who's Actually Holding the IOUs."* A follow-up: *"What if foreigners stopped buying?"*

### 2. Japan's debt puzzle
1. **What it is:** government debt of **about 250% of GDP** (IMF), the highest among rich countries.
2. **How it works:**
   - The debt is in yen and mostly held domestically. The **Bank of Japan owns roughly half** of government bonds after years of QE.
   - The rest is held largely by Japanese banks, insurers and pension funds.
   - Interest rates were near or below zero from the late 1990s until 2024. The BoJ ended negative rates in **March 2024**.
3. **Why it matters:** it tests the idea that high debt must cause a crisis.
4. **What makes it interesting:** a paradox. The biggest debtor among rich countries has had some of the lowest borrowing costs.
5. **What viewers should understand:** who holds the debt, what currency it's in, and what the central bank does matter more than the size. The open question is **[debated]**: what happens as rates rise and the population shrinks?
6. **Story:** *"Japan Owes 2.5 Years of Its Economy. Why Hasn't It Collapsed?"*

### 3. Sri Lanka's 2022 collapse
1. **What it is:** a balance-of-payments and debt crisis, meaning the country ran out of the foreign currency it needed to pay for imports and debts.
2. **What happened:**
   - The 2019 Easter bombings and then COVID hit tourism.
   - Large tax cuts in 2019 shrank government revenue.
   - A sudden **ban on chemical fertiliser** (April 2021) cut harvests.
   - Foreign reserves drained, and fuel, food and medicine shortages followed.
   - The country defaulted on foreign debt (April–May 2022).
   - Mass protests forced President Gotabaya Rajapaksa out (July 2022).
   - An IMF programme followed (March 2023), along with a long restructuring with China, India, Japan and bondholders.
3. **Why it matters:** it shows how quickly a middle-income country can unravel, and how modern multi-creditor debt deals work.
4. **What makes it interesting:** several policy choices compounded each other, and there were dramatic scenes (the presidential pool, fuel queues).
5. **What viewers should understand:** crises usually come from **stacked shocks plus policy mistakes**, not one villain. "Chinese debt trap" explanations are **[debated]**. China was one creditor among several.
6. **Story:** *"How Sri Lanka Ran Out of Money in 18 Months."*

### 4. Ireland's GDP illusion
1. **What it is:** Ireland's GDP per person looks like one of the highest in the world, but that partly reflects **multinational companies' accounting**.
2. **How it works:**
   - Ireland's low corporate tax rate (12.5%, moving to 15% for large firms under the 2021 OECD global minimum tax deal) attracted the European headquarters of tech and pharma firms.
   - In **2015**, Irish GDP was reported to have grown about **26%**, largely because firms (notably Apple) moved intellectual property to Ireland. The economist Paul Krugman called it "**leprechaun economics**".
   - Ireland created **GNI\*** (modified gross national income) to measure the domestic economy more realistically.
3. **Why it matters:** it shows that the headline statistic can mislead, which is a core expert skill.
4. **What makes it interesting:** a "rich" country where many young people face a severe housing shortage and emigrate.
5. **What viewers should understand:** always ask what the statistic actually measures.
6. **Story:** *"Ireland Isn't as Rich as You Think."* (The Invisible Hand covered a similar angle and got 91K, so you'd need a fresh angle, e.g. housing + tax + the Apple tax case.)

### 5. Poland's catch-up
1. **What it is:** one of Europe's biggest economic success stories since 1990.
2. **How it works:**
   - **"Shock therapy"** (the Balcerowicz Plan, 1990) quickly freed prices and opened trade, with painful early years.
   - Joining the **EU (2004)** opened markets and brought large EU funds (Poland became the largest net recipient of EU funds) and FDI (German supply chains).
   - It was the **only EU economy to avoid recession in 2009**.
   - GDP per person in PPP terms rose from roughly half the EU average around 2004 to about four-fifths by the early 2020s. (Check Eurostat.)
3. **Why it matters:** proof that convergence (poorer countries catching up) can happen.
4. **What makes it interesting:** the contrast with stagnating Britain and Italy; emigrants returning; and its cautious banks and floating currency during 2008.
5. **What viewers should understand:** success had several causes (institutions, the EU, geography, education, the złoty's flexibility), and challenges remain: demographics and wages still below Western levels.
6. **Story:** *"How Poland Quietly Caught Up With Western Europe."*

### 6. Argentina's century of decline
1. **What it is:** a country among the world's richest per person in the early 1900s (according to historical estimates such as the Maddison Project) that later suffered repeated crises.
2. **What happened:**
   - Recurring high inflation, **nine sovereign defaults** (the most recent in 2020), currency controls and political swings, including Peronism and military rule.
   - The 2001 crisis: a fixed peso–dollar peg collapsed, followed by a default and bank freezes (the *corralito*).
   - Javier Milei was elected in late 2023 and began sharp spending cuts and a devaluation. Monthly inflation fell sharply during 2024, while poverty rose before easing.
   - The long-run outcome is **[debated]** and still unfolding.
3. **Why it matters:** the classic case study of how policy and institutions can waste natural wealth.
4. **What makes it interesting:** rich land and educated people, yet a cycle of crises; a radical reformer's experiment happening live.
5. **What viewers should understand:** there is no single cause. Inflation, deficits financed by money printing, mistrust of the peso and political cycles reinforce each other.
6. **Story:** *"Argentina Was Richer Than France. What Went Wrong?"* (Verify the France comparison for the specific year you use.)

### 7. South Korea's fertility collapse
1. **What it is:** the world's lowest fertility rate: **about 0.72 in 2023**, ticking up to about 0.75 in 2024 (Statistics Korea).
2. **How it works:**
   - Common explanations include very high housing costs in Seoul, intense education competition and spending on private tutoring (*hagwon*), long working hours, gender-role pressures and job insecurity.
   - The government has spent **hundreds of trillions of won** on pro-birth policies since 2006, with limited results.
3. **Why it matters:** it will shrink the workforce and strain pensions. It is a preview for other ageing countries.
4. **What makes it interesting:** an "unsolvable" puzzle. Money hasn't fixed it.
5. **What viewers should understand:** fertility is driven by economics *and* culture. Single-cause stories (e.g. blaming feminism or one policy) misrepresent the research. Present several factors.
6. **Story:** *"Why South Korea Can't Pay Its Way Out of a Baby Crisis."* (Dr. Jonathan Tam's 6.7M Japan video shows the demand. Stay evidence-led and avoid sensationalism.)

### 8. Norway vs Venezuela: two oil stories
1. **What it is:** two oil-rich countries with opposite outcomes.
2. **How it works:**
   - **Norway** put oil revenue into a sovereign wealth fund (first deposit 1996). It spends only around **3%** of the fund each year (its fiscal rule), and the fund is now worth over **$1.5 trillion**.
   - **Venezuela** has the world's largest proven oil reserves. It relied on oil revenue for spending, then oil production and prices fell, money was printed, price controls were imposed and hyperinflation followed. Its economy shrank by roughly three-quarters in the 2010s by some estimates. Mass emigration followed (millions of people, per the UN).
3. **Why it matters:** the "resource curse" isn't destiny. Institutions and rules decide the outcome.
4. **What makes it interesting:** a clean comparison with a moral.
5. **What viewers should understand:** Dutch disease, saving vs spending, and institutional quality. Venezuela also faced US sanctions from the late 2010s, so include that **[debated]** factor fairly.
6. **Story:** *"Same Oil, Opposite Fates: Norway vs Venezuela."*

### 9. Germany's broken model
1. **What it is:** Germany's export-manufacturing model under pressure.
2. **How it works:**
   - For decades the model combined **cheap Russian gas**, **booming Chinese demand** (for cars and machinery) and **US security**.
   - After 2022, Russian gas was cut, energy costs rose and Chinese carmakers became competitors.
   - GDP shrank in both **2023 and 2024**.
   - In **March 2025**, Germany loosened its constitutional "debt brake" for defence spending and created a **€500 billion** infrastructure fund.
3. **Why it matters:** Europe's largest economy is reinventing itself.
4. **What makes it interesting:** the fall of the "model student". It mirrors The Invisible Hand's 1.2M-view German video.
5. **What viewers should understand:** the structural issues (energy, demographics, bureaucracy, under-investment) versus the cyclical ones.
6. **Story:** *"How Germany Lost Its Three Superpowers."*

### 10. China's property crisis
1. **What it is:** a slump in China's real-estate sector, which with related industries was estimated at roughly a quarter of GDP (the Rogoff & Yang estimate is about 29%).
2. **How it works:**
   - Developers borrowed heavily and pre-sold flats before building them.
   - In 2020, the government's "**three red lines**" rules restricted developer borrowing.
   - **Evergrande** defaulted (2021) and was ordered into liquidation by a Hong Kong court (January 2024). Country Garden also defaulted (2023).
   - Prices fell, unfinished flats sparked mortgage boycotts, and local governments, which relied on land sales, lost revenue.
3. **Why it matters:** most Chinese household wealth is in property, and the slump weighs on consumption and growth.
4. **What makes it interesting:** ghost cities and unfinished towers make striking visuals, and a slow-motion crisis raises "is this China's Japan moment?" **[debated]**.
5. **What viewers should understand:** the difference between a sudden crash and a managed, slow deflation; and the role of local government finance.
6. **Story:** *"The $X Trillion Problem Hiding in China's Empty Towers"* (fill X from a verified estimate).
