## PART 5: How we got here, a timeline in 9 eras

### Era 1: Before 1914, empires and the gold standard
- Many currencies were **backed by gold**: you could swap notes for a fixed amount of gold.
  - *Gold standard:* money's value is tied to gold, which limits how much can be printed.
- Britain was the financial centre. The pound sterling was the dominant world currency.
- Colonial empires shaped trade. Raw materials flowed to Europe and manufactured goods flowed out.
- **Why it matters for videos:** much of today's wealth gap, borders and trade routes traces back here (e.g. "Why is Latin America still poor?").

### Era 2: 1914–1945, war, hyperinflation and depression
- **World War I** destroyed the gold standard; governments printed money to fund war.
- **Germany 1923:** hyperinflation. Prices doubled every few days at the peak.
- **1929 Wall Street Crash leading to the Great Depression (1930s).** Banks failed, trade collapsed and unemployment soared. Countries raised tariffs (the US Smoot–Hawley tariff of 1930), which made it worse.
- **Keynes** argued governments should spend during slumps, which became modern fiscal policy.
- **Lesson experts quote constantly:** depressions + protectionism + political extremism reinforced each other.

### Era 3: 1944–1971, the Bretton Woods system
- **1944, Bretton Woods (New Hampshire, USA):** the Allies designed the post-war system.
  - The US dollar was fixed to gold at **$35 per ounce**, and other currencies were pegged to the dollar.
  - Two institutions were created: the **IMF** (stabilise currencies and lend in crises) and the **World Bank** (fund reconstruction and development).
- **1947, GATT:** cutting tariffs; it later became the WTO in 1995.
- **The Marshall Plan** (from 1948) helped rebuild Western Europe.
- **The "golden age":** fast growth in Western Europe and Japan (the German *Wirtschaftswunder*, "economic miracle"; Japan's high-growth era).

### Era 4: 1971–1979, the dollar floats and oil shocks
- **15 August 1971, the "Nixon Shock":** the US ended dollar-to-gold convertibility. Currencies began to **float**, and money became **fiat**.
  - *Fiat money:* money with value because a government declares it legal tender and people trust it, not because of gold.
- **1973 oil embargo:** OPEC's Arab members cut supply and oil prices roughly quadrupled. **1979** brought a second oil shock after the Iranian revolution.
- **The petrodollar:** from the mid-1970s, Saudi oil was priced in dollars and its profits recycled into US assets.
- **"Stagflation":** stagnation + inflation together. High inflation with weak growth confused economists.

### Era 5: 1979–1991, high rates, debt crises and markets
- **The Volcker shock (1979–82):** the US Fed under Paul Volcker raised rates to around 20%, which broke inflation but caused recession.
- **The Latin American debt crisis (1982):** Mexico and others couldn't repay dollar loans when US rates spiked. This became the "lost decade" in Latin America.
- **Thatcher (UK) and Reagan (US):** privatisation, deregulation, lower taxes, weaker unions.
- **1985, the Plaza Accord:** rich countries agreed to weaken the dollar. The yen rose sharply, which fed **Japan's asset bubble**.
- **1989–91:** the Berlin Wall fell and the Soviet Union collapsed. Eastern Europe began "shock therapy" transitions to market economies.

### Era 6: 1990–2007, globalisation
- **Japan's bubble burst (1990–91),** leading to the "lost decade(s)" of slow growth and deflation.
- **1992, Maastricht Treaty:** the path to the euro. The **euro launched in 1999**, and cash arrived in 2002.
- **Crises in emerging markets:** Mexico 1994, **Asia 1997** (it began in Thailand in July 1997), Russia 1998, Argentina 2001 (default and peso crisis).
- **China joined the WTO in 2001,** becoming "the world's factory". Manufacturing shifted from the West, the so-called "China shock".
- **The EU expanded east in 2004:** Poland, the Baltic states, Czechia and others joined.
- **Cheap credit and housing booms:** the US, Spain, Ireland and the UK.

### Era 7: 2007–2012, the Global Financial Crisis and the eurozone crisis
- The US subprime mortgage collapse came first.
  - *Subprime* = loans to borrowers with weak credit.
- **15 September 2008, Lehman Brothers went bankrupt,** setting off a global panic.
- Central banks cut rates to near zero and launched **QE**. Governments bailed out banks.
- **The eurozone debt crisis (2010–12):** Greece (bailouts from 2010, restructuring in 2012), Ireland (2010 bailout), Portugal (2011), Spain (2012 bank rescue), Cyprus (2013).
- **July 2012:** ECB president Mario Draghi said the ECB would do "**whatever it takes**" to save the euro. Spreads fell, and this is one of the most quoted lines in finance.

### Era 8: 2013–2019, cheap money and rising rivalry
- **Zero or negative interest rates** in Europe and Japan.
  - *Negative rate:* banks pay to park money at the central bank.
- **2013:** China launched the **Belt and Road Initiative**, financing ports, railways and roads worldwide.
- **2014:** Russia annexed Crimea and was hit by its first sanctions.
- **2015:** Greece's referendum and third bailout.
- **2016:** the Brexit referendum (23 June) and Trump's first election.
- **2018:** the US–China trade war began.

### Era 9: 2020–today, shocks and fragmentation
- **2020, COVID:** the deepest global recession since the 1930s, met by huge stimulus and QE.
- **2021–23:** an inflation surge (from supply chains, stimulus, and the energy shock after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022) and the fastest rate rises in decades.
- **2022:** Russia's central-bank reserves frozen; the UK gilt crisis (September); Sri Lanka's default; Europe's gas crisis.
- **2021 onward:** China's property crisis. Evergrande defaulted in 2021, and a Hong Kong court ordered its liquidation in January 2024.
- **2023:** Silicon Valley Bank failed; BRICS agreed to expand (new members joined from 2024); Javier Milei was elected in Argentina (November).
- **2024:** Japan ended negative interest rates (March), its first rate rise in 17 years.
- **2025:** large new US tariffs; Germany loosened its constitutional "debt brake" for defence and created a €500 billion infrastructure fund (March); Moody's downgraded the US.

### Today's big themes (these are facts about current debates, not predictions)
- High government debt across rich countries, with higher interest costs than in the 2010s.
- **"De-dollarisation" [debated]:** some countries seeking alternatives to the dollar, with central banks buying gold heavily since 2022. The dollar remains dominant.
- **Fragmentation:** trade blocs, tariffs, "friendshoring".
- **Ageing populations** in East Asia and Europe.
- **The energy transition** and the race for critical minerals.
- AI and productivity hopes.

### What may happen next **[predictions: label them in videos]**
- More countries breaking 100% debt-to-GDP, and possible bond-market "vigilante" episodes like the UK's in 2022.
- A slow, partial shift of reserves away from the dollar, not a sudden collapse (the majority expert view).
- Labour shortages in ageing countries and fiercer competition for migrants.
- India and Southeast Asia gaining manufacturing share.
- Climate-driven costs and migration.

> **Rule:** in a script, say "economists expect", "forecasts suggest" or "one scenario is" for anything about the future. Never say "this will happen".

## PART 6: The players and how they connect

### The relationship map (simple version)
```
            Voters ──elect──▶ GOVERNMENTS ──appoint/mandate──▶ CENTRAL BANKS
               ▲                 │  │  ▲                          │
               │          taxes/spend  │ borrow (bonds)            │ set interest rates,
               │                 │  ▼  │                          │ buy bonds (QE)
          HOUSEHOLDS ◀──jobs── COMPANIES   BOND INVESTORS ◀───────┘
               │                 ▲          (pension funds, banks,
               │ savings         │ FDI       foreign central banks)
               ▼                 │               ▲
            BANKS ───lend───▶ everyone      RATING AGENCIES grade risk
                                                 │
   IMF / WORLD BANK ──emergency loans + conditions──▶ governments in crisis
   OPEC+ ──oil supply/prices──▶ everyone
   GREAT POWERS (US, China, EU, Russia) ──trade, sanctions, alliances──▶ everyone
```

### Key institutions

| Player | Who/what | What they do | Why they matter / relationships |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IMF** (International Monetary Fund) | 190+ member countries, based in Washington DC, founded 1944 | Monitors economies, lends to countries in crisis, publishes the *World Economic Outlook* | The "lender of last resort" for countries. Its loans come with conditions (often austerity or reforms), which makes it controversial. Biggest voting shareholder: the US. |
| **World Bank** | Sister institution of the IMF | Long-term development loans and grants; huge data library | Funds infrastructure and poverty programmes. Its data (World Development Indicators) is a key research source. |
| **Central banks**: Federal Reserve (US), ECB (euro area), Bank of England, Bank of Japan, People's Bank of China | Mostly independent public institutions | Set interest rates, control the money supply, supervise banks, act as "lender of last resort" | Their decisions move mortgages, currencies and markets worldwide. The Fed's moves affect every country with dollar debt. |
| **BIS** (Bank for International Settlements) | Basel, Switzerland; the "central bank for central banks" | Coordinates central banks, sets bank rules (the "Basel" capital rules), publishes data | An excellent data source on debt and banking. |
| **OECD** | A club of 38 mostly rich democracies, Paris | Research, statistics, policy advice; ran the global minimum corporate-tax deal | A great source for comparing rich countries (housing, productivity, pensions). |
| **WTO** | World Trade Organization, Geneva | Trade rules and dispute settlement | Weakened since its appeals body stopped functioning in 2019. |
| **G7 / G20** | Clubs of major economies | Coordinate policy in summits | The G20 (including China, India, Brazil and others) coordinated the 2008 crisis response. |
| **BRICS** | Originally Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa; expanded from 2024 (members include Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, the UAE and Indonesia; Saudi Arabia's status has been ambiguous) | A forum for non-Western powers, plus the New Development Bank | Often presented as "the alternative to the West". Members have very different interests, so check claims. |
| **OPEC / OPEC+** | Oil producers led by Saudi Arabia; "+" includes Russia | Coordinate oil output to influence prices | Their decisions move inflation worldwide. |
| **EU / European Commission** | A union of 27 countries; the Commission is its executive | Single market, trade policy, budget rules, recovery funds | Its funds helped transform Poland; its rules shaped Greece's austerity. |
| **Rating agencies**: S&P, Moody's, Fitch | Private companies | Grade debt risk | Downgrades can raise borrowing costs. They were criticised for rating subprime bonds AAA before 2008. |
| **Paris Club** | Group of creditor governments | Negotiates debt relief | China often isn't a member, which complicates restructurings (e.g. Zambia, Sri Lanka). |

### Market players
- **Bond investors** (pension funds, insurers, banks, hedge funds, foreign central banks): decide the price at which governments can borrow. "Bond vigilantes" is the nickname for investors who punish reckless policy by selling bonds.
- **Sovereign wealth funds** (Norway's GPFG, Saudi Arabia's PIF, Abu Dhabi's ADIA, Singapore's GIC): huge state investors that buy companies and property worldwide.
- **Commercial banks:** create most money in practice by lending. A new loan creates a new deposit.
- **Multinational companies:** decide where factories and profits go. Example: Ireland's GDP is inflated by multinationals' accounting, so Ireland created a special measure, **GNI\***, to show the "real" domestic economy.
- **Big asset managers** (e.g. BlackRock, Vanguard): manage trillions in savings, so they own slices of most listed companies.

### People worth knowing (historical influence)
- **Adam Smith (1723–1790):** *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). His "invisible hand" is the idea that self-interest in markets can benefit society. Your reference channel is named after this.
- **John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946):** governments should spend to fight slumps. He co-designed Bretton Woods.
- **Milton Friedman (1912–2006):** monetarism; "inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon". He influenced Thatcher and Reagan.
- **Friedrich Hayek (1899–1992):** markets and prices as information systems; a critic of central planning.
- **Paul Volcker (Fed chair 1979–87):** crushed US inflation with high rates.
- **Deng Xiaoping (China's leader from 1978):** "reform and opening up", which began China's growth miracle.
- **Lee Kuan Yew (Singapore's PM 1959–90):** transformed Singapore after independence in 1965.
- **Park Chung-hee (South Korea, 1961–79):** state-led industrial push. It was authoritarian, so present both the growth and the repression.
- **Mario Draghi (ECB president 2011–19):** "whatever it takes".
- **Modern names you'll meet:** central bank chiefs (whoever currently chairs the Fed, ECB, Bank of England and Bank of Japan, so check the current holder), IMF managing directors, and finance ministers in crisis countries.

### How the players interact: one example
**"Why did Sri Lanka's crisis in 2022 involve China, India, the IMF and bondholders at once?"**
1. Sri Lanka borrowed from bondholders (international sovereign bonds), China (infrastructure loans, e.g. Hambantota port) and others.
2. It ran out of dollars and defaulted (April–May 2022).
3. India provided emergency credit lines.
4. The IMF agreed a bailout in 2023, but only after creditors gave "financing assurances".
5. China, bondholders and Paris Club creditors each had to agree to restructuring terms.

**Lesson:** modern debt crises are **multi-creditor negotiations**, and China's role as a lender is a real story angle. The popular "debt-trap diplomacy" claim is **[debated]**: researchers disagree about intent.
