## PART 1: What this niche means

### The simple explanation (as if you've never heard of it)

Imagine a street with 200 houses.
- Some families on the street get richer every year.
- Some get poorer.
- Some borrow too much money.
- Some lend money to everyone else.
- Some families argue, some cooperate, and a few control the street's only shop.

**Countries behave like those families.** This niche tells the story of countries' money: who is getting richer, who is getting poorer, who owes whom, and why.

It answers everyday questions people already ask:
- "Why can't I afford a house when my parents could?"
- "Why are prices going up everywhere?"
- "Why is everyone leaving my country?"
- "How can the US owe trillions and still be fine?"
- "Why did Poland get richer while Britain stalled?"

So a video in this niche is basically: **"Here is a country (or the whole world). Here is something strange or worrying happening to its money or its people. Here is the real reason why, and what happens next."**

### The deeper professional explanation

Professionally, this niche sits where three subjects overlap:

| Field | What it studies | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| **Macroeconomics** | The economy as a whole: growth, inflation, unemployment, interest rates, government debt | "Why is Japan's debt so high but its interest rates so low?" |
| **Political economy** | How politics and economics shape each other: policy choices, elections, institutions | "Why did Argentina keep defaulting?" |
| **Geopolitics** | How geography and power shape relations between countries: trade, wars, alliances, resources | "Why does the US care about Greenland?" |

**New words used above, explained right away:**
- *Macro* means big. Macroeconomics is about the whole economy of a country, not one shop or one person. *Microeconomics* is about individual firms and households.
- *Default* means failing to pay back a debt on time.
- *Institutions* are the "rules of the game": courts, central banks, property rights, how honest the government is.

### Why the niche exists and why people watch

1. **Personal stakes.** Rent, wages, prices and jobs are all macroeconomics, felt personally.
2. **Confusion.** The news reports *what* happened ("inflation hits 9%") but rarely explains *why* in plain language.
3. **Anxiety and curiosity about the future.** Is a crisis coming? Is my country declining?
4. **Identity and comparison.** People love comparing their country with others.
5. **Hidden-system curiosity.** "Who actually holds all this debt?" has the same appeal as a mystery story.

### The main purpose
To turn complicated, abstract systems (money, debt, trade, power) into **clear stories with causes, people and consequences**, so viewers feel they finally *understand* the world.

### Questions the niche tries to answer
- Why do some countries get rich and others stay poor?
- Why do economic "miracles" happen, and why do they end?
- Why do prices, rents and house prices rise faster than wages?
- How does government debt work, and when does it become dangerous?
- What gives a currency value, and why do currencies collapse?
- How do wars, sanctions, oil and trade routes move money around the world?
- What happens to a country when its population shrinks or ages?
- Who wins and who loses from a policy?

### What viewers expect
- A **clear question** in the first 30 seconds
- **Maps, charts and real numbers**
- **A surprising explanation**, not the obvious one
- **Fairness**, not pure propaganda. This audience is sceptical and the comments will correct you.
- **A payoff**: "so this is why, and this is what comes next"

## PART 2: The vocabulary you must own

Each entry gives:
- **Simple:** the meaning in plain English
- **Pro:** the professional meaning
- **Example:** a real-world case
- **In a video:** how creators use the word
- **Sentence:** a correct sentence you can reuse

The terms are grouped so that each group builds on the one before.

### Group A: Measuring an economy

**1. Economy**
- **Simple:** all the buying, selling, making and working that happens in a place.
- **Pro:** the system of production, distribution and consumption of goods and services in a region.
- **Example:** Germany's economy is built heavily on making and exporting cars and machinery.
- **In a video:** "Germany's economy is built on one idea: make things and sell them abroad."
- **Sentence:** "Ireland's economy changed completely when tech companies arrived."

**2. GDP (Gross Domestic Product)**
- **Simple:** the total value of everything a country produces in a year. It's like the country's "yearly earnings".
- **Pro:** the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a period.
- **Example:** if a country makes 100 cars at $20,000 each and nothing else, its GDP is $2 million.
- **In a video:** "Poland's GDP has more than doubled since it joined the EU."
- **Sentence:** "GDP grew 3%, but most people didn't feel any richer."

**3. GDP per capita**
- **Simple:** GDP divided by the number of people, so the average "earnings" per person. *Per capita* is Latin for "per head".
- **Pro:** a standard rough measure of average living standards.
- **Example:** China's total GDP is huge, but per person it is still far below the US.
- **In a video:** "China is the world's second-largest economy, but per person, it's still a middle-income country."
- **Sentence:** "Luxembourg has one of the highest GDP-per-capita figures in the world."

**4. PPP (Purchasing Power Parity)**
- **Simple:** an adjustment for how much things cost locally. $1 buys more lunch in Vietnam than in Switzerland.
- **Pro:** a conversion rate that equalises the purchasing power of different currencies.
- **Example:** a haircut costs $3 in one country and $30 in another; PPP corrects for that.
- **In a video:** "Adjusted for local prices (economists call this PPP), the gap is much smaller."
- **Sentence:** "Measured in PPP terms, Poland is closer to Britain than most people think."

**5. Recession**
- **Simple:** a period when the economy shrinks and people lose jobs.
- **Pro:** often defined as two consecutive quarters (three-month periods) of falling GDP. In the US, the official judgement is made by a committee (the NBER).
- **Example:** most rich countries went into recession in 2008–09.
- **In a video:** "Poland was the only EU country that avoided recession in 2009."
- **Sentence:** "Economists warned the tariffs could push the country into recession."

**6. Depression**
- **Simple:** a very deep, very long recession.
- **Pro:** no strict definition. It means a severe, prolonged collapse in output and employment.
- **Example:** the Great Depression of the 1930s.
- **In a video:** "This wasn't a recession. It was a depression."
- **Sentence:** "Greece's GDP fell by about a quarter, a depression-scale collapse."

**7. Productivity**
- **Simple:** how much a worker produces in an hour.
- **Pro:** output per hour worked (labour productivity). It is the main long-run driver of wages.
- **Example:** a farmer with a tractor produces far more per hour than one with a hand tool.
- **In a video:** "Britain's real problem isn't immigration or Brexit alone. It's flat productivity since 2008."
- **Sentence:** "Wages can't rise for long unless productivity rises."

**8. Living standards / cost of living**
- **Simple:** how comfortably people can live / how much basic life costs.
- **Pro:** often measured with real wages, disposable income, and housing costs as a share of income.
- **Example:** Lisbon rents rose much faster than Portuguese wages.
- **In a video:** "Portugal's cost-of-living crisis is really a housing crisis."
- **Sentence:** "Real living standards fell for two years in a row."

### Group B: Prices and money

**9. Inflation**
- **Simple:** prices going up over time, so your money buys less.
- **Pro:** the rate of increase in the general price level, usually measured by the CPI.
- **Example:** if bread costs £1.00 this year and £1.10 next year, that's 10% inflation on bread.
- **In a video:** "Inflation hit double digits for the first time in 40 years."
- **Sentence:** "High inflation quietly cuts the value of everyone's savings."

**10. CPI (Consumer Price Index)**
- **Simple:** a "shopping basket" the government prices every month to measure inflation.
- **Pro:** a weighted index of the prices of a representative basket of consumer goods and services.
- **Example:** the basket includes food, rent, fuel and clothes.
- **In a video:** "Official CPI says 3%, but rent is rising 10%."
- **Sentence:** "Food has a bigger weight in the CPI of poorer countries."

**11. Deflation**
- **Simple:** prices falling. It sounds good, but it can be dangerous.
- **Pro:** a sustained fall in the general price level. People delay buying, debts become heavier, and growth stalls.
- **Example:** Japan had long periods of mild deflation after the 1990s.
- **In a video:** "Japan's problem was the opposite of everyone else's: prices wouldn't rise."
- **Sentence:** "Deflation makes debts harder to repay, because wages fall but the debt doesn't."

**12. Hyperinflation**
- **Simple:** prices rising so fast that money becomes almost worthless.
- **Pro:** often defined as inflation above 50% per month.
- **Example:** Germany in 1923; Zimbabwe in 2008; Venezuela in the late 2010s.
- **In a video:** "In 1923, Germans used banknotes as wallpaper."
- **Sentence:** "Hyperinflation usually starts when a government prints money to pay its bills."

**13. Real vs nominal**
- **Simple:** *nominal* = the raw number. *Real* = the number after removing inflation.
- **Pro:** real values are adjusted for price changes.
- **Example:** your pay rises 5% (nominal), but prices rise 7%, so your **real** pay fell about 2%.
- **In a video:** "Wages are up in nominal terms, but down in real terms."
- **Sentence:** "Always compare real wages, not nominal wages."

**14. Currency / exchange rate**
- **Simple:** a currency is a country's money; the exchange rate is how much of one currency you get for another.
- **Pro:** the exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. It can float (set by markets) or be pegged (fixed by the government).
- **Example:** if £1 = $1.25, a $25 book costs £20.
- **In a video:** "The rupiah hit a record low against the dollar."
- **Sentence:** "A weaker currency makes imports more expensive."

**15. Devaluation / depreciation**
- **Simple:** your currency losing value.
- **Pro:** *devaluation* is a deliberate cut to a fixed (pegged) currency's value. *Depreciation* is a market-driven fall.
- **Example:** Egypt devalued its pound several times in 2022–24.
- **In a video:** "Overnight, everyone's savings lost a third of their value."
- **Sentence:** "Depreciation helps exporters but hurts importers and anyone with foreign debts."

**16. Peg**
- **Simple:** tying your currency to another at a fixed rate.
- **Pro:** a fixed exchange-rate regime, defended by the central bank using reserves.
- **Example:** the Hong Kong dollar is pegged to the US dollar; Saudi Arabia's riyal too.
- **In a video:** "Hong Kong's dollar has been pegged to the US dollar since 1983."
- **Sentence:** "Defending a peg can drain a central bank's reserves fast."

**17. Reserve currency**
- **Simple:** the money other countries' governments hold as "savings".
- **Pro:** a currency held in large amounts by central banks as part of their foreign-exchange reserves. The US dollar is dominant, at a bit under 60% of reserves according to IMF data in the mid-2020s.
- **Example:** India's central bank holds US dollars to defend the rupee.
- **In a video:** "The dollar isn't just America's money. It's the world's savings account."
- **Sentence:** "Being the reserve currency lets the US borrow more cheaply."

**18. Petrodollar**
- **Simple:** the system where oil is mostly priced and sold in US dollars.
- **Pro:** this arrangement, rooted in 1970s US–Saudi agreements, created constant global demand for dollars and the recycling of oil revenues into US assets.
- **Example:** a country buying oil needs dollars first.
- **In a video:** "Every barrel of oil sold in dollars strengthens America's position."
- **Sentence:** "Some analysts argue the petrodollar is weakening as more oil trades in yuan." (This is an argument, not an established fact.)

### Group C: Banks, interest and central banks

**19. Interest rate**
- **Simple:** the price of borrowing money.
- **Pro:** the percentage charged on a loan, or paid on savings, per year.
- **Example:** borrow £1,000 at 5% and you owe £50 a year in interest.
- **In a video:** "When interest rates jumped, mortgage payments doubled."
- **Sentence:** "Higher interest rates cool down borrowing and spending."

**20. Central bank**
- **Simple:** the "bank of the government" that controls a country's money supply and main interest rate.
- **Pro:** a public institution responsible for monetary policy, financial stability and (often) an inflation target.
- **Example:** the Federal Reserve (US), the European Central Bank (euro area), the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan.
- **In a video:** "The Bank of England raised rates 14 times in a row."
- **Sentence:** "The central bank's job is to keep inflation near 2%."

**21. Monetary policy vs fiscal policy**
- **Simple:**
  - *Monetary* = the central bank controlling money and interest rates.
  - *Fiscal* = the government controlling taxes and spending.
- **Pro:** these are the two main levers of macroeconomic management.
- **Example:** cutting interest rates is monetary policy; cutting taxes is fiscal policy.
- **In a video:** "The government spent (fiscal) while the central bank tightened (monetary). They were pulling in opposite directions."
- **Sentence:** "Fiscal policy is decided by politicians; monetary policy by the central bank."

**22. Quantitative easing (QE)**
- **Simple:** the central bank creating new money to buy bonds, which pushes interest rates down.
- **Pro:** large-scale asset purchases used when interest rates are already near zero.
- **Example:** the Fed, the Bank of England, the ECB and the Bank of Japan all used QE after 2008 and in 2020.
- **In a video:** "After 2008, central banks created trillions in new money. That's quantitative easing."
- **Sentence:** "QE made borrowing cheap, which also pushed up house and stock prices."

**23. Bank run**
- **Simple:** everyone rushing to take money out of a bank at once because they fear it will fail.
- **Pro:** a self-fulfilling crisis. Banks lend out most deposits, so they can't repay everyone at once.
- **Example:** Northern Rock (UK, 2007); Silicon Valley Bank (US, 2023).
- **In a video:** "In 2023, Silicon Valley Bank lost $42 billion in deposits in one day."
- **Sentence:** "Deposit insurance exists to stop bank runs."

### Group D: Government debt

**24. Budget deficit vs debt**
- **Simple:**
  - *Deficit* = spending more than you earn **this year**.
  - *Debt* = the **total** you owe from all the years added up.
- **Pro:** the deficit is a flow; the debt is a stock.
- **Example:** you overspend £1,000 this year (deficit), and your total credit-card balance is £10,000 (debt).
- **In a video:** "The deficit is how fast the hole gets deeper. The debt is the size of the hole."
- **Sentence:** "Cutting the deficit doesn't reduce the debt. It just slows its growth."

**25. Debt-to-GDP ratio**
- **Simple:** how big a country's debt is compared with its yearly earnings.
- **Pro:** the standard measure of a government's debt burden.
- **Example:** Japan's government debt is roughly 2.5× its GDP (about 250%), the highest among rich countries.
- **In a video:** "Japan owes two and a half years of its entire economy."
- **Sentence:** "A debt-to-GDP ratio above 100% isn't automatically a crisis. It depends who you owe and at what interest."

**26. Bond / government bond**
- **Simple:** an IOU. You lend the government money, and it promises to pay you back later with interest.
- **Pro:** a debt security. Country-specific names include US Treasuries, UK gilts, German Bunds and Japanese JGBs.
- **Example:** you buy a 10-year UK gilt for £100; the government pays you interest every year and £100 back after 10 years.
- **In a video:** "When you put money in a pension, part of it is lent to governments through bonds."
- **Sentence:** "Pension funds are some of the biggest buyers of government bonds."

**27. Bond yield**
- **Simple:** the effective interest rate a bond pays, which tells you how risky investors think the borrower is.
- **Pro:** the return on a bond given its current market price. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions.
- **Example:** if investors fear a government won't repay, they demand higher yields.
- **In a video:** "Italy's bond yields jumped, a sign markets were losing faith."
- **Sentence:** "Rising yields mean borrowing is getting more expensive for the government."

**28. Spread**
- **Simple:** the gap between two countries' borrowing costs.
- **Pro:** usually measured against a safe benchmark, e.g. Italian vs German 10-year yields.
- **Example:** in 2011–12, Italian and Spanish spreads over Germany widened sharply.
- **In a video:** "Watch the spread. It's the market's fear gauge."
- **Sentence:** "France's spread over Germany widened during its political crisis."

**29. Credit rating**
- **Simple:** a grade (like AAA, BB) saying how likely a borrower is to repay.
- **Pro:** opinions issued by agencies such as S&P, Moody's and Fitch. A downgrade can raise borrowing costs.
- **Example:** the US lost its top rating from S&P in 2011, from Fitch in 2023 and from Moody's in 2025.
- **In a video:** "Moody's stripped the US of its last AAA rating."
- **Sentence:** "A downgrade to 'junk' status shuts some investors out completely."

**30. Default / restructuring**
- **Simple:** *default* = failing to pay. *Restructuring* = renegotiating to pay less or later.
- **Pro:** a sovereign default is a government missing its debt payments; restructuring often involves a "haircut" (lenders accept less).
- **Example:** Argentina has defaulted many times (including 2001 and 2020); Sri Lanka defaulted in 2022; Greece restructured in 2012.
- **In a video:** "In 2022, Sri Lanka ran out of dollars and stopped paying its debts."
- **Sentence:** "After the default, the country was locked out of borrowing for years."

**31. Bailout**
- **Simple:** being rescued with emergency loans.
- **Pro:** financial assistance from governments or institutions (often the IMF), usually with conditions attached.
- **Example:** Greece received three bailouts between 2010 and 2015.
- **In a video:** "The bailout came with a price: deep spending cuts."
- **Sentence:** "Ireland accepted an EU–IMF bailout in 2010."

**32. Austerity**
- **Simple:** a government cutting spending and/or raising taxes to reduce its deficit.
- **Pro:** fiscal consolidation. It is controversial because it can deepen recessions.
- **Example:** the UK after 2010; Greece under its bailout programmes.
- **In a video:** "Ten years of austerity left hospitals and councils stretched."
- **Sentence:** "Critics argue austerity made the recession longer."

### Group E: Trade and the world economy

**33. Exports / imports / trade balance**
- **Simple:** *exports* = selling to other countries; *imports* = buying from them; the trade balance is the difference.
- **Pro:** a trade surplus means exports exceed imports; a trade deficit means imports exceed exports.
- **Example:** Germany and China have run large surpluses; the US runs large deficits.
- **In a video:** "Germany sells far more to the world than it buys."
- **Sentence:** "A trade deficit isn't automatically bad, but a permanent one needs financing."

**34. Current account**
- **Simple:** a wider version of the trade balance, including income and transfers (like money migrants send home).
- **Pro:** goods, services, primary income and secondary income. A deficit must be financed by borrowing or selling assets to foreigners.
- **Example:** before 1997, Thailand ran big current-account deficits funded by short-term foreign loans, and then they fled.
- **In a video:** "When a country spends more abroad than it earns, someone has to lend it the difference."
- **Sentence:** "A large current-account deficit made the country vulnerable when investors panicked."

**35. Tariff**
- **Simple:** a tax on imported goods.
- **Pro:** it raises the domestic price of imports. It protects some domestic producers and is usually paid largely by domestic buyers.
- **Example:** US tariffs on Chinese goods from 2018, expanded heavily in 2025.
- **In a video:** "Tariffs on China didn't bring factories home. Many moved to Vietnam."
- **Sentence:** "Economists generally find most of a tariff's cost is passed to local consumers."

**36. Supply chain**
- **Simple:** all the steps and countries a product passes through before you buy it.
- **Pro:** the network of suppliers, manufacturers and logistics.
- **Example:** an iPhone uses parts from many countries and is assembled mainly in Asia.
- **In a video:** "When one factory in Taiwan stops, car plants in Germany go silent."
- **Sentence:** "COVID exposed how fragile global supply chains were."

**37. Sanctions**
- **Simple:** economic punishments one country puts on another.
- **Pro:** restrictions on trade, finance, travel or assets, used as a foreign-policy tool.
- **Example:** after 2022, Western countries froze around $300 billion of Russia's central-bank reserves.
- **In a video:** "Sanctions were supposed to collapse Russia's economy. Here's why they didn't."
- **Sentence:** "Sanctions often reroute trade rather than stop it."

**38. Commodity**
- **Simple:** a basic raw material that is traded, like oil, wheat, copper or gas.
- **Pro:** standardised goods traded globally, with prices set in world markets.
- **Example:** when oil prices fall, Saudi Arabia's budget suffers.
- **In a video:** "Russia's economy is still, at its core, a commodity exporter."
- **Sentence:** "Commodity-dependent countries ride a rollercoaster of boom and bust."

**39. Dutch disease**
- **Simple:** when finding a valuable natural resource accidentally damages the rest of the economy.
- **Pro:** resource exports push up the currency and wages, making manufacturing and other exports uncompetitive. The term was coined by *The Economist* in 1977 about the Netherlands' gas discoveries.
- **Example:** some oil states struggle to build other industries.
- **In a video:** "Discovering oil made the country poorer in the long run. Economists even have a name for it."
- **Sentence:** "Norway avoided Dutch disease partly by saving its oil money in a sovereign wealth fund."

**40. FDI (Foreign Direct Investment)**
- **Simple:** foreign companies building or buying businesses in your country.
- **Pro:** a long-term investment with a controlling interest, e.g. a factory, unlike short-term "portfolio" investment in stocks and bonds.
- **Example:** Asian and German carmakers building plants in Mexico or Hungary.
- **In a video:** "Foreign factories poured into Poland after it joined the EU."
- **Sentence:** "FDI brings jobs and technology, but profits may flow abroad."

### Group F: People, development and power

**41. Demographics / fertility rate**
- **Simple:** *demographics* = facts about a population (ages, births, migration). *Fertility rate* = the average number of children per woman.
- **Pro:** a total fertility rate of about 2.1 is "replacement level", the rate that keeps a population stable without migration.
- **Example:** South Korea's fertility rate fell to roughly 0.7 in 2023, the lowest in the world.
- **In a video:** "At this rate, 100 South Korean adults would have about 36 children and only about 13 grandchildren." (How it works: 100 adults include about 50 women; 50 × 0.72 ≈ 36 children; about 18 of them are women; 18 × 0.72 ≈ 13.)
- **Sentence:** "Falling fertility means fewer workers paying for more retirees."

**42. Dependency ratio**
- **Simple:** how many non-workers (kids and retirees) each worker supports.
- **Pro:** dependants (usually under 15 and over 64) divided by the working-age population.
- **Example:** Japan's ratio is among the highest in the world.
- **In a video:** "Soon, every Japanese worker will support almost one retiree."
- **Sentence:** "A rising dependency ratio puts pressure on pensions and healthcare."

**43. Brain drain / emigration**
- **Simple:** educated people leaving their country for better pay abroad.
- **Pro:** net emigration of skilled workers, which reduces the home country's growth potential.
- **Example:** large numbers of young Greeks, Portuguese and Irish left during their crises.
- **In a video:** "The country trained its doctors, and then exported them."
- **Sentence:** "Brain drain can become self-reinforcing."

**44. Middle-income trap**
- **Simple:** countries that grow from poor to middle-income but then get stuck.
- **Pro:** a stall in convergence when cheap-labour advantages fade before innovation-led growth starts. The concept is debated among economists.
- **Example:** often discussed for Malaysia, Thailand, Brazil and South Africa.
- **In a video:** "Getting from poor to middle-income is easy compared with the next step."
- **Sentence:** "Avoiding the middle-income trap requires better education and institutions."

**45. Geopolitics**
- **Simple:** how geography and power affect what countries do.
- **Pro:** the study of the influence of geography, resources and power on international relations.
- **Example:** Taiwan's location and its chip industry make it geopolitically critical.
- **In a video:** "Look at the map and the strategy suddenly makes sense."
- **Sentence:** "Greenland matters geopolitically because of Arctic routes and minerals."

**46. Soft power / hard power**
- **Simple:** *hard* = force and money; *soft* = attraction and influence (culture, ideas, reputation).
- **Pro:** these are Joseph Nye's terms for coercive vs attractive power.
- **Example:** South Korea's K-pop and films are soft power.
- **In a video:** "China lends money. America exports Hollywood. Both are buying influence."
- **Sentence:** "Soft power takes decades to build and years to lose."

**47. Sovereign wealth fund**
- **Simple:** a country's investment piggy bank.
- **Pro:** a state-owned investment fund, often financed by commodity revenues or surpluses.
- **Example:** Norway's fund (over $1.5 trillion), and Saudi Arabia's PIF, which finances NEOM.
- **In a video:** "Norway saved its oil money. Others spent theirs."
- **Sentence:** "The sovereign wealth fund owns a slice of almost every big company in the world."

**48. Bubble**
- **Simple:** when prices rise far above what something is really worth, driven by hype, and then crash.
- **Pro:** an asset-price boom detached from fundamentals (the underlying value).
- **Example:** Japan's late-1980s property and stock bubble; the US housing bubble before 2008.
- **In a video:** "At the peak, the land under Tokyo's Imperial Palace was said to be worth more than all of California." (This is a famous claim. Present it as "reportedly".)
- **Sentence:** "Cheap credit inflated the bubble; rising rates popped it."

**49. Austerity vs stimulus**
- **Simple:** *stimulus* = the government spending more or cutting taxes to boost the economy; the opposite of austerity.
- **Pro:** expansionary fiscal policy.
- **Example:** the US stimulus payments in 2020–21.
- **In a video:** "America chose stimulus; Europe chose caution. Here's how it played out."
- **Sentence:** "Stimulus can boost growth but may add to inflation and debt."

**50. Structural vs cyclical**
- **Simple:** *cyclical* = a temporary up-and-down; *structural* = a deep, long-lasting problem.
- **Pro:** cyclical problems follow the business cycle; structural ones come from the economy's makeup (demographics, productivity, institutions).
- **Example:** a recession is cyclical; Japan's ageing population is structural.
- **In a video:** "This isn't a bad year. It's a structural problem."
- **Sentence:** "Structural unemployment won't fix itself when the economy recovers."

> **Expert habit:** when you hear a new term, ask three questions:
> 1. Is it a **flow** (per year) or a **stock** (a total)?
> 2. Is it **real** or **nominal**?
> 3. Is it **per person** or **total**?
>
> Most beginner errors come from mixing these up.

## QUIZ 1 (send me your answers; no answer key on purpose)

**Level 1: Basics**
1. In one sentence, what is this niche about?
2. What is the difference between a budget **deficit** and government **debt**? Use a household example of your own.
3. Your salary rises 4% and inflation is 6%. Did your **real** income rise or fall, and by roughly how much?

**Level 2: Understanding**

4. Why can a *weaker* currency be good news for some people in a country and bad news for others? Name one winner and one loser.
5. Explain in plain English why bond yields rise when investors get scared about a government.
6. What's the difference between monetary policy and fiscal policy? Give one example of each.

**Level 3: Applying**

7. A country discovers huge oil reserves. Ten years later its factories have closed. Which term explains this, and how does it happen?
8. China's total GDP is the world's second largest, yet a YouTube commenter says "China is still a poor country." Which two terms from this module would you use to explain why both statements can be true?

**Level 4: Thinking like an expert**

9. Japan has government debt of about 250% of GDP and hasn't had a debt crisis. Greece had a crisis with far less. Without looking anything up, give two possible reasons why. (Hint: think about *who* is owed and *what currency* the debt is in.)
10. Write one sentence you could say in a video that correctly uses **three** of these words: *structural, productivity, real wages*.
