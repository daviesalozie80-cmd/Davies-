## PART 10: How to research and verify

### Source ladder (most to least reliable for facts)

| Tier | Source type | Examples | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1: Primary data** | Official statistics and international databases | **IMF DataMapper / World Economic Outlook database**, **World Bank Data**, **OECD Data Explorer**, **Eurostat**, **FRED** (US Federal Reserve data), national statistics offices (ONS UK, Destatis Germany, INE Spain/Portugal, Statistics Korea), central bank websites, **BIS** statistics, US Treasury **TIC data** (who holds US debt), **UN Comtrade** (trade), **Our World in Data** (charts built on primary sources, with links) | Every number in your script |
| **2: Expert analysis** | Central bank speeches and reports, IMF country reports, OECD *Economic Surveys*, academic papers (NBER, SSRN, VoxEU/CEPR), think tanks (Bruegel, PIIE, IFS, Brookings; note their leanings) | Explanations of causes and debates |
| **3: Quality journalism** | Financial Times, Reuters, Bloomberg, The Economist, WSJ, AP, Nikkei Asia | Timelines, quotes, context, news pegs |
| **4: Secondary/aggregator** | Wikipedia, explainers | **Starting points only.** Follow their citations to Tier 1–2. |
| **5: Unreliable for facts** | YouTube creators (including your competitors), TikTok, X/Twitter threads, Reddit, blogs with no sources | Idea discovery and "what people believe" **only** |

> **Rule:** every statistic in your script must trace to Tier 1 (or Tier 2 if no primary data exists). Never take a number from a competitor's video.

### Spotting misleading information
- **No date or source** on a statistic.
- **Cherry-picked time frames:** the chart starts at a convenient peak or trough.
- **Truncated y-axis:** a small change made to look dramatic.
- **Nominal vs real** confusion.
- **Total vs per person** confusion (China vs Luxembourg).
- **Single-cause stories** ("It's all because of X").
- **Emotional language standing in for evidence:** "collapse", "destroyed", "doomed" with no numbers.
- **Anonymous "experts say".**
- **Old data presented as current.**
- **Exchange-rate illusions** (GDP "falling" because the currency fell).

### Cross-checking: the rule of two
1. Find the number in **two independent Tier 1–2 sources**. For example, the IMF and the national statistics office.
2. If they differ, find out **why**: different definitions (gross vs net debt), dates, or currency.
3. Use the most authoritative source and **name it on screen** ("Source: IMF, Oct 2026").

### Verifying dates and statistics
- **Dates:** check against the original announcement (the central bank's press release, an official gazette, a court record) or two quality news outlets.
- **Statistics:**
  - Note the **definition** (e.g. general government gross debt).
  - Note the **unit** (% of GDP, US$, local currency, constant or current prices).
  - Note the **period** (year, quarter, month).
  - Note the **release date and revisions**.
- **Records** ("highest ever", "first time since"): check how far back the data goes. "Highest since records began in 1997" is honest; "highest ever" may not be.

### Reading research papers and reports without drowning
1. Read the **abstract** (summary) and **conclusion** first.
2. Look at the **charts and tables**. Most papers have one key figure.
3. Find the **research question** and **data** (which country? which years?).
4. Note the **limitations** section. That's where honest caveats live.
5. Check **who funded it** and whether it was peer-reviewed. (*Peer review* = checked by other experts before publication.)
6. For IMF and OECD reports: read the "**Key messages**" or "**Executive summary**", then the chapter on your topic.

### Facts, opinions, theories, predictions
- **Fact:** verifiable.
- **Theory:** an explanation supported by evidence but still testable (e.g. why the middle-income trap exists).
- **Opinion:** an evaluation.
- **Prediction:** a forecast. Even IMF forecasts are often wrong, so present them as forecasts.

### Avoiding misinformation
- Use attribution language every time ("according to…", "estimates suggest…").
- Separate "what happened" from "why it happened" from "what's next".
- Correct errors publicly: a pinned comment and a description update.
- Don't use edited clips out of context, fake quotes or AI-generated "real" images.

### Your pre-script research process (use it for every video)
1. **Question (15 min):** write the single question the video answers.
2. **Background (1–2 hrs):** read the Wikipedia overview, then **follow its citations**. Read one long-form article (FT, Economist or Reuters).
3. **Data pull (1–2 hrs):** gather 10–25 key numbers from Tier 1. Record each in a **fact sheet** with columns: claim · number · unit · date · source link · second source.
4. **Mechanism (1 hr):** draw the cause chain (Module 2 systems). Mark which arrows are proven vs debated.
5. **Counter-view (30 min):** find the strongest argument *against* your thesis.
6. **Human angle (30 min):** find real quotes or case studies, or design an illustrative character clearly based on real data.
7. **Freshness check (15 min):** search news from the last 30 days on your topic.
8. **Final check:** every number in the script must appear in the fact sheet with a source. Put the main sources in the video description.

## PART 11: Turning knowledge into scripts

### The structure (works for 12–20 minute videos)

| Section | Length | Job | Writing tips |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Hook** | 0:00–0:30 | Shock fact or paradox, then the core question | Lead with the most surprising **verified** number. Don't say "In this video we'll…". |
| **2. Stakes and promise** | 0:30–1:15 | Why it matters to the viewer; preview that the answer is surprising | "And the answer isn't what most people think." |
| **3. Context** | 1–4 min | How we got here: a short history, a map, one key chart | Only the history needed for the explanation |
| **4. Cause 1** | 4–8 min | The first mechanism: claim → number → visual → consequence | One idea per 60–90 seconds |
| **5. Re-hook** | ~8 min | "But that's not the real problem…" | Opens a new loop |
| **6. Causes 2–3** | 8–14 min | Escalate to the biggest cause | Save the most counter-intuitive cause for last |
| **7. Reveal** | 14–17 min | The twist: who benefits, why it's hard to fix, the surprise winner | The "aha" moment |
| **8. What's next** | 17–19 min | Scenarios (best, base, worst), clearly labelled as predictions | Answer the title question explicitly |
| **9. Close** | last 20–30 s | A one-line takeaway | End fast; don't drag the outro |

### Writing a strong hook: 4 proven types
1. **Paradox:** "Japan owes more than any rich country on Earth. So why are its borrowing costs among the lowest?"
2. **Shock number:** "In 2023, South Korea's fertility rate fell to about 0.7. At that rate, 100 adults today would have about 36 children, and only about 13 grandchildren."
3. **Scene:** "In April 2022, Sri Lankans were queuing for hours, some for days, to buy fuel. Three months later, protesters were swimming in the president's pool."
4. **Question everyone has:** "If every country is in debt… who is all that money owed to?"

### Explaining complex ideas simply: the "ladder" method
1. **Everyday analogy first:** "A bond is an IOU."
2. **Then the real term:** "Economists call this a government bond."
3. **One number:** "Japan has issued IOUs worth about 2.5 times its yearly economy."
4. **One comparison:** "That's more than twice the US level relative to its economy." (Check the current ratio before saying this.)
5. **One consequence:** "So even a small rise in interest rates costs Japan enormous amounts."

**Rules:**
- **One new term per minute at most.**
- **Repeat key terms the same way every time.** Don't switch between "debt", "borrowing" and "liabilities".
- **Round numbers on screen** (about $36 trillion), but keep the exact figure in your fact sheet.
- **Always compare numbers** to something human ("that's €4,000 per citizen").
- **Visualise every number** (a map, a chart, an object metaphor).

### Creating curiosity and tension
- **Open loops:** "We'll come back to why that number matters."
- **List promises:** "There are three reasons. The third explains everything."
- **Escalation:** order causes from smallest to biggest.
- **Countdown or clock:** "Sri Lanka had weeks of fuel left."
- **Counter-intuitive turns:** "Here's the strange part…"

### Introducing people and events
- **Name + role + one human detail:** "Mario Draghi, the head of the European Central Bank, a quiet technocrat who would say the three words that saved the euro."
- **Events: date + place + what changed.** "15 September 2008: Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy, and the panic went global."

### A satisfying conclusion
- Return to the opening question and **answer it directly**.
- Show the **trade-off** (there's rarely a perfect solution).
- Leave **one honest open question**.
- Optionally tie back to the viewer ("so next time your rent goes up…").

### Preventing confusion and overwhelm
- **Signpost:** "So far: cheap money and tourism. Now the third piece."
- **Recap every 4–5 minutes in one sentence.**
- **Cut anything that doesn't serve the core question,** even if it's interesting. Save it for another video.
- **A word count of about 150 words per minute.** A 15-minute video is about 2,250 words.

## PART 13: Common beginner mistakes

| # | Mistake | Why people make it | Why it's harmful | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Copying numbers from other YouTubers | Fast and convenient | Errors spread, and you can't defend them | Tier 1 sources only, with a fact sheet |
| 2 | Single-cause explanations | Simpler and more dramatic | Wrong, and the comments will destroy credibility | Use the "3–5 forces" decomposition |
| 3 | Doom without evidence ("collapse!") | It gets clicks | Trust erodes; advertiser suitability suffers | Use strong *questions* in titles and evidence in scripts |
| 4 | Mixing real and nominal, total and per person | Not knowing the difference | Misleading comparisons | Run the 3 expert questions on every number |
| 5 | Out-of-date data | Old sources rank high in search | An expert audience notices | Date every number; do the freshness check |
| 6 | Too much jargon | Trying to sound smart | Viewers leave | One new term per minute, each explained |
| 7 | Too little substance | Fear of being boring | Feels shallow; no rewatch or share | Give each section one number + one mechanism |
| 8 | Political bias presented as fact | Personal views | Alienates half the audience; misinforms | Present the main sides; label opinions |
| 9 | Predictions stated as certainties | Confidence sounds authoritative | Looks foolish later | Use scenarios: "if X, then Y" |
| 10 | Covering a topic only because it's trending | Chasing views | Thin research, rushed errors | Run the 18/25 scoring filter |
| 11 | Ignoring the counter-argument | Confirmation bias | Comments supply it for you, publicly | Include a "the case against" section |
| 12 | Blaming groups of people (migrants, an ethnicity) | Easy villains | Harmful, inaccurate, monetisation risk | Analyse systems and policies, not groups |
| 13 | Over-long history sections | Research feels valuable | Retention drops | Use only the history the explanation needs |
| 14 | No human element | Data feels like enough | Abstract and forgettable | Give every video one real or clearly illustrative person |
| 15 | Not answering the title's question | Running out of time | Viewers feel cheated | Write the answer *first*, then the script |
