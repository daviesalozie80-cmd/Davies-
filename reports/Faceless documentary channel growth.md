# Build slowly, because three-month explosions rarely happen

**Short answer: it is possible but very unlikely.** A new faceless, AI-voiced documentary channel about true-life conspiracy events launching in November 2026 has, in my estimate, **roughly a 15–25% chance of reaching 1,000 subscribers within 90 days, about 3–5% of reaching 10,000, and well under 1% of reaching 100,000 or more**. The odds of being approved for ad revenue in that window are about 1–3%, mainly because new applicants from **February 1, 2027 need 8,000 watch hours, double the old 4,000**. The niche has real demand: faceless and host-led mystery documentary channels such as Fern, LEMMiNO and The Why Files each have about 5–6 million subscribers. But the leaders took years to get there, the median recent long-form channel needed **about 46 uploads and about 16 months to reach 1,000 subscribers**, and YouTube spent 2025–2026 removing AI-template channels from monetization. A "conspiracy event in every continent" series is a good format, and I found no established competitor running it. It will only work if every video is properly researched and visibly crafted. Narravox can help you draft, but it should never be your source of facts. The realistic goal for the first 90 days is to prove that the format can produce one breakout video and to learn from the data, not to "blow up". A caution on the evidence: **almost every figure here comes from search-engine snippets, third-party trackers or vendor blogs that sell faceless-channel tools.** None of the research could open YouTube's own Help pages in full, so check exact policy wording before you rely on it.

## The niche has 5-million-subscriber demand but no verified overnight AI success

Demand for mystery and true-story documentaries is not in doubt. **Fern**, a faceless animated "armchair documentary" channel covering unsolved crimes, hacking and espionage, has about **5M+ subscribers** ([CreatorDB](https://creatordb.app/creatorstats/fern-tv/)). **LEMMiNO** has about **5.93M from only 171 videos** ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/UCRcgy6GzDeccI7dkbbBna3Q)). **The Why Files** has about **5.99M subscribers and 1.28B views** ([CreatorDB](https://creatordb.app/creatorstats/thewhyfiles/)). **Barely Sociable** built about **1.38M subscribers on just 36 videos** ([Social Blade](https://socialblade.com/youtube/handle/barelysociable/videos)). The lesson from that last number is that in this genre, a small number of excellent videos beats a flood of average ones.

These are your competitors for viewers' time, and none of them is a new AI-voiced channel. Most are built around a named human host and years of compounding growth. The Why Files reportedly had only **about 5,000 subscribers in its first year** before it broke through ([Builtbyfoundry](https://www.builtbyfoundry.io/blog/aj-gentile-why-files-6m-subscribers-334-book-reading-list)). Fern has writers, motion designers and a dedicated fact-checking team ([Fern about page](https://www.watchfern.com/about)). Vendor blogs call the unsolved-mysteries niche "very saturated with top channels dominating" ([Autonolab](https://blog.autonolab.com/niches/2025-12-28-faceless-youtube-unsolved-mysteries/)).

The fast-growth stories you will see online do not hold up well. The often-shared "261,000 subscribers in 5 months" faceless case study was publicly called unproven by a rebuttal that says the author "does not provide any proof" and is selling a course ([Medium rebuttal](https://medium.com/@ratefrog/this-article-is-a-scam-and-a-lie-850653b36a03)). A "100k in 90 days" claim is a single promotional video with no stated niche ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PVsqCXZ3sRQ)). The research found **no named, verifiable 2024–2026 faceless conspiracy channel that reached 10k–100k within months**. The best documented AI-hosted success, Chloe VS History, took **about 15 months and 52 videos to reach about 356K subscribers** ([tugan.ai](https://tugan.ai/blog/how-to-start-a-faceless-youtube-channel-with-ai)). Vendor guides that want to sound optimistic still put a realistic 0-to-100k timeline at **12–14 months**, and call faceless YouTube "a 12–24 month bet" ([Frameco](https://frameco.app/blog/how-to-grow-faceless-youtube-channel-0-to-100k-subscribers); [Frameloop](https://frameloop.ai/blog/faceless-youtube-statistics-2026)).

The AI documentary channels that did grow fast are warnings, not models. "True Crime Case Files" used ChatGPT to write **fabricated murder stories presented as real**, got nearly 2M views on its top video, and was **terminated in February 2025** ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2025/02/14/youtube-true-crime-case-files-ai-crime-channel-terminated/)). AI "boring history to sleep to" channels scaled cheaply, and historians have since attacked them for inaccuracy ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/ai-generated-boring-history-videos-are-flooding-youtube-and-drowning-out-real-history/)).

### A continent-by-continent format is open but untested

The strongest thing about your idea is the structure. The research found **no established "conspiracy from every continent" series** ([search context](https://rosetta.to/u/wendigoon/page/2)). The dominant formats are the single deep-dive and Wendigoon's "iceberg", whose 21-part Conspiracy Theory Iceberg passed **16M views** ([Rosetta](https://rosetta.to/u/wendigoon/the-conspiracy-theory-iceberg)). Sources selling into the true-story space repeatedly name international and non-English cases as underserved in English ([White Glove Content](https://www.whiteglovecontent.com/niches/true-crime); [imagine.art](https://www.imagine.art/blogs/how-to-start-true-crime-youtube-channel)), and Real Stories' cartel documentaries show that Latin American stories have an audience ([White Glove Content](https://www.whiteglovecontent.com/niches/true-crime)). There are two catches. First, these "underserved" claims come without data, so the gap is a hypothesis. Second, African, Asian and South American stories tend to have less English-language source material, which means more fact-checking work, and they may draw fewer high-paying US and UK viewers. A practical answer is to lead with globally recognizable stories in each region and then branch into lesser-known ones once you see which videos people watch.

## YouTube rewards viewer satisfaction, not effort, and new channels get small tests

YouTube describes its recommendation system as "pulling" videos for each viewer based on predicted satisfaction. It learns from clicks, watch time, likes, shares, viewer surveys and negative signals such as "Don't recommend channel" ([Search Engine Journal](https://beta.searchenginejournal.com/how-youtubes-recommendation-system-works-in-2025/538379/); [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9962575)). For a beginner, this means YouTube does not owe you views for uploading. Third-party guides describe each new video being shown to a small test audience and expanded only if people click and keep watching ([TubeBuddy](https://www.tubebuddy.com/blog/how-to-get-discovered-on-youtube-why-new-creators-are-being-pushed-in-2026/); [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/how-youtube-algorithm-recommends-videos)). YouTube itself has not published that mechanism or any numeric thresholds.

Two official numbers help you judge your early videos. Half of all channels have an impressions click-through rate (CTR) **between 2% and 10%**, and new channels swing more widely than that ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/7628154)). Studio's intro marker shows how many viewers are still watching at **30 seconds**, and a guide citing YouTube Help treats **50% or more** as above typical ([Teleprompter](https://www.teleprompter.com/blog/youtube-audience-retention)). The widely repeated "you need 70%" rule has no good source. A newer feature called **Hype** lets viewers boost videos from channels under 500K subscribers during the first 7 days, with extra weight for small channels ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/trending/youtube-algorithm-in-2026-month-by-month-changes-that-affect-your-views)). That makes early loyal viewers more valuable than usual.

Growth benchmarks are sobering. vidIQ's study of 200K channels created in 2025 found a median of **46 uploads to reach 1,000 subscribers for mostly long-form channels**, versus 90 for Shorts-first and 108 for hybrid channels ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-uploads-1000-subscribers/)). The median time to 1,000 is **about 16 months** for channels created after 2023 ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-subscriber-growth-statistics/)). If you upload once a week, you will have about 13 videos at day 90, roughly a quarter of the median. Hitting 1,000 subscribers by then means beating the typical channel by a wide margin, which is possible only if one video takes off.

## The policies that can quietly end an AI conspiracy channel

This is the section most beginners skip, and it matters more than the algorithm.

### Inauthentic content is the biggest single risk

On **July 15, 2025**, YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" rule to **"inauthentic content"**. The examples it targets include **channels that upload narrated stories that differ only superficially, and slideshows with the same narration** ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-clarifies-monetization-update-inauthentic-repeated-content/752892/)). That describes exactly the "AI story generator plus voice plus stock images" pipeline. YouTube said AI voices are **not banned** in themselves. Around **July 2026** it clarified three categories: generic or template-based content, **off-putting or distressing content**, and AI personas on sensitive topics. Channels "dedicated to" any of these are removed from the Partner Program **whether or not AI made them** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/youtube-clarifies-policies-around-ai-slop-and-upsetting-videos/)). Reviewers judge the **whole channel**: its videos, titles, descriptions and how the content was made ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/youtube-hacks/reused-content-monetization-rejected)).

Enforcement is real. In January 2026, **16 of the top 100 AI-slop channels** on a Kapwing list disappeared, representing about **35M subscribers** ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-ai-slop-crackdown-2026/)). Coverage of a Google paper describes a system that terminated **about 130,000 channels in six months** by flagging groups of accounts that reuse the same AI template ([Fstoppers](https://fstoppers.com/artificial-intelligence/how-google-machine-terminated-130000-ai-slop-youtube-channels-six-months-903645)). Human-made faceless channels have reported being caught up in it ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/youtube-ai-slop-crackdown-faceless-creators-collateral-damage)). For you, this means three things. Every video needs a visibly different structure and original research. You should not share templates or assets with other channels you run. And a channel that is nothing but grim tragedy in the same format could trip the "distressing" category.

If you are rejected, third-party guides report that you can appeal within 21 days, or fix the channel and reapply after 30 days. They recommend keeping research notes, script drafts and editing timelines as evidence that you made the work yourself ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/youtube-hacks/reused-content-monetization-rejected)). Start keeping those records from video one.

### "True-life conspiracy" is safe only when it is documented history

Since January 2019, YouTube has reduced recommendations of **"borderline" content that could misinform users in harmful ways**. Its own examples were miracle cures, flat earth, and **blatantly false claims about historic events like 9/11** ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/continuing-our-work-to-improve); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2019/01/25/youtube-recommendations-conspiracy-videos-google-pledges-tweak-them)). A US test cut recommendation views of such content by **more than 50%**. The videos stay up but stop being recommended. That is fatal for a new channel that depends on recommendations to be found. The research found no 2025–2026 update on how strictly this is enforced now.

The safe lane, and the one your brief already describes, is **documented conspiracies**: declassified operations, proven cover-ups and state deceptions that courts, governments or historians have confirmed. That is history and journalism, not misinformation. The danger zone is endorsing unproven claims about health, vaccines, elections, 9/11 or real victims. The Why Files' creator says the channel tries to back claims with facts and debunk myths where possible ([Podcastle](https://podcastle.ai/blog/the-why-files)), and that evidence-first framing is what keeps the big channels both recommended and monetized. Brand the channel as documentary or history, not as "conspiracy".

### Ads: non-graphic and factual is monetizable, gore is not

YouTube's advertiser guidelines say documentary coverage of a historic event can earn ads. Content focused on blood or violence "without other context" cannot, and "appearing to profit from a tragic event with no discernible benefit to users" is a listed concern ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6162278?hl=en)). A **January 2026 update** widened full ad eligibility for non-graphic, dramatized coverage of controversial issues such as abuse and self-harm, though child abuse content stays excluded ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/16/youtube-relaxes-monetization-guidelines-for-some-controversial-topics/)). Practitioners say graphic imagery in the **thumbnail or first ~15 seconds** routinely triggers limited ads ([Thumblore](https://www.thumblore.com/blogs/true-crime-youtube-thumbnails)). Many conspiracy events involve massacres, assassinations or experiments on people, so keep those parts suggestive rather than explicit.

### AI labels and copyright

You must tick the "altered or synthetic content" box in Studio for **realistic** AI visuals that a viewer could mistake for a real person, place or event, such as a photorealistic AI "reenactment". A generic AI narrator does not need a label, but **a synthetic voice imitating a real person does** ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content)). Since **May 2026**, YouTube auto-applies the label if it detects undisclosed photorealistic AI. It says a label alone **does not change recommendations or monetization** ([YouTube Blog](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/improving-ai-labels-viewers-creators/)), so disclosing honestly costs you nothing. Never synthesize the voice of a real victim. Netflix drew a backlash for doing that in its Gabby Petito series ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/gabby-petito-netflix-docuseries-ai-recreatiion-backlash-rcna193185)). For footage, remember that "fair use" does not stop automated claims: a 97-minute documentary got a copyright strike from FOX5 for about **one minute** of news clips ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2024/04/03/fox-station-copyright-strikes-youtubers-documentary-over-use-of-1-minute-clip)). The lowest-risk visuals are original maps, timelines and motion graphics, followed by public-domain government archives and licensed stock.

## Monetization: the 8,000-hour bar puts ad money beyond 90 days

YouTube's Partner Program (YPP) has two levels. The **lower tier**, which unlocks memberships, Super Thanks and Shopping but **no ad revenue**, needs **500 subscribers, 3 uploads in 90 days, and 3,000 watch hours in 12 months** (or 3M Shorts views in 90 days). The **full tier**, which unlocks ad revenue, currently needs **1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours** or 10M Shorts views ([Tella](https://www.tella.com/blog/how-to-join-youtube-partner-program); [AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/monetization/youtube-partner-program-requirements-2026-the-complete-guide)). According to an announcement on August 10, 2026, **from February 1, 2027, new applicants need 8,000 watch hours in the past 365 days or 20M Shorts views in 90 days**, still with 1,000 subscribers. The lower tier is unchanged and existing partners are grandfathered ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/417147/youtube-doubles-eligibility-requirements-for-creat.html); [Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2026/08/10/youtube-doubles-eligibility-requirements-partner-program/)). I could not read YouTube's own announcement, so confirm this in Studio.

Because review takes about a month, the practical deadline to qualify under the old bar is **around December 31, 2026** ([Prepublish.ai](https://prepublish.ai/blog/youtube-monetization-requirements-2027)). A channel launching in late November 2026 will not have 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 hours within five weeks, so **plan on the 8,000-hour bar**. Here is the arithmetic, which is mine rather than sourced. 8,000 hours is 480,000 minutes. That equals **24,000 views at a 20-minute average view duration, 48,000 at 10 minutes, or about 96,000 at 5 minutes**. A 15-minute documentary from a new channel typically keeps a viewer for a fraction of its length, so expect to need roughly 60,000–100,000 long-form views in total. Shorts views do not count toward watch hours.

Once you are monetized, expect modest earnings. Vendor estimates put **conspiracy content at roughly $2–8 RPM** (what you earn per 1,000 views), mystery and unsolved at about $4–9, and **documentary and dark history at about $5–12**. One small audit of 31 faceless documentary channels averaged **$12.6** ([FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/blog/is-conspiracy-theories-a-good-youtube-niche-in-2026-rpm-and-growth-data); [FluxNote mystery](https://fluxnote.io/blog/is-mystery-a-good-youtube-niche-in-2026-rpm-and-growth-data); [Autonolab](https://blog.autonolab.com/niches/2025-11-29-faceless-youtube-documentary/)). True-crime Shorts earn only about **$0.03–0.12 RPM** ([FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/youtube-shorts-rpm-true-crime-niche)). Even OutlierKit admits there is no reliable universal niche RPM benchmark ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/youtube-rpm-benchmarks/)). For planning, assume **$4–8 per 1,000 long-form views**, lower if many viewers are outside the US, UK, Canada and Australia. In that case, 100,000 views is worth about $400–800. Calling the channel "documentary" rather than "conspiracy" likely helps both ad suitability and RPM.

## Narravox is a drafting toy, not a research tool

"Narravox AI" (narravoxai.com) describes itself as an AI storytelling assistant that "helps you instantly generate stories, narration, and scene prompts for animated or faceless videos". It has free and premium plans, and its genres are reportedly **"True Life" and "Sci-Fi"** with sub-genres ([Narravox AI](https://www.narravoxai.com/)). The research **could not confirm a continent or location selector**, who makes it, the price tiers, or its commercial-use terms. ScamAdviser gives the site a **slightly low trust score** and lists a Delaware address ([ScamAdviser](https://www.scamadviser.com/check-website/narravoxai.com)). Several unrelated products share the name, including a Devpost hackathon project and a Quebec voice-over business ([Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/narravox); [narravox.com](https://narravox.com/)), so make sure you are looking at the right one.

The core problem is that an AI "True Life" generator with no cited sources will invent names, dates, quotes and events, because that is what language models do when they are asked to tell a story. If you publish that as a documentary of real conspiracy events, you are making "false claims about historic events", which is the borderline category, and you are repeating the True Crime Case Files mistake. Running every video through the same generator and voice also matches YouTube's own example of "narrated stories that differ only superficially". Tool-driven, research-free production is exactly what vendors themselves blame for AI faceless videos getting no views ([overseeros](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/ai-faceless-videos-get-no-views)).

### A safe AI workflow

Use AI to speed up the parts of the work that are not facts. Start each video by researching the real event yourself. Build a **source log** of primary documents (declassified files, court records, government reports, contemporary newspapers) plus **at least two independent secondary sources for each key claim**. Only then ask Narravox or another AI tool to suggest structure, transitions or a cold-open draft. Check the draft line by line against your log, and delete anything you cannot source. Rewrite it in your own channel voice, and change the structure from video to video (chronological, investigation-style, "three theories tested", and so on) so that no two uploads feel templated. Put your sources in the description. Use one premium, consistent AI voice tuned for pacing; cheap robotic voices reportedly drive viewers away, while good ones are less of a problem, though that evidence is anecdotal ([viralfaceless.io](https://viralfaceless.io/blog/ai-voiceover-faceless-channel-that-doesnt-sound-like-slop-2026)). Add a short note in your About section saying the narration is AI-voiced and the research is human-checked. Disclose realistic AI reenactments, and never clone a real person's voice. This takes longer, but it is the one approach that protects you from the inauthentic-content rules, the misinformation rules and comment-section debunkers at the same time.

## Packaging, length and cadence decide whether the test audience expands

Paddy Galloway, a well-known YouTube strategist, puts it bluntly: if you cannot give an idea a clickable title and thumbnail, "it's not an idea anymore". He recommends designing several title and thumbnail options before you make the video ([Creator Science](https://podcast.creatorscience.com/paddy-galloway/)). Small true-crime channels show what strong packaging can do without a subscriber base. A 6,040-subscriber channel got **175K views (41x its normal)** with "The Case That Made Police Physically Sick", and a 7,500-subscriber channel got **103K (20x)** with "These Cases Are Darker Than Anyone Could Imagine" ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/faceless-youtube-growth-strategy/)). Rise-and-fall and "How X became Y" framings work for documentary channels too; "How Rockefeller Built His Trillion Dollar Oil Empire" got about 9.7x its channel's typical views ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/channel/businesscasual)). Adapted to your niche, the patterns become "The Government Experiment [Country] Tried to Bury", "How One Journalist Exposed [Operation]", and "[Number] Years Later, the Files Were Finally Opened". Because viewer satisfaction feeds the algorithm, the video has to deliver what the title promises. Keep thumbnails non-graphic: one striking object, document or map plus two or three words.

For hooks, open cold on the most concrete moment of the story within 5 seconds, set up the tension, and promise where the video is going by 30 seconds ([Onira](https://onira.studio/guides/youtube-documentary-hooks)). Skip the logo intro, because Galloway blames branded intros for early exits ([Colin and Samir](https://www.colinandsamir.com/resources/the-new-rules-of-youtube-from-paddy-galloway)). After each upload, check the retention graph and cut whatever caused the dips ([FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/faceless-youtube-channel-first-90-days-plan)). No source gives a proven best length. Comparable narrated documentaries such as MagnatesMedia run **10–20 minutes** ([faceless.my](https://faceless.my/youtube/faceless-business-documentary-channels/)), so start at **12–20 minutes**. That is long enough to build watch hours and fit mid-roll ads later, and short enough to keep the script tight.

On cadence, Fern posts about weekly ([sigmastory](https://sigmastory.in/fern-proves-high-end-3d-animation-and-documentaries-are-the-future-of-youtube/)). Vendors push 2–3 videos a week and warn against bursts followed by silence ([FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/faceless-youtube-channel-first-90-days-plan)). For a beginner doing real research, **one long-form video a week** is the sustainable maximum. Add **2–3 Shorts per video, cut from its most gripping moment and posted within 3 days**, since one channel's data showed that related Shorts posted close to the long-form release drove more long-form views ([Spotter](https://spottercommunity.beehiiv.com/p/increase-views-from-related-shorts)). Don't expect much from Shorts on their own, though: Shorts viewers generally convert poorly to long-form ([Subscribr](https://subscribr.ai/youtube-strategy/youtube-shorts-vs-long-form-growth)), and Shorts-first channels needed twice as many uploads to reach 1,000 subscribers. Budget, as my estimate: **$50–150 a month in tools** and **15–30 hours per video** if you make everything yourself, or $150–500 per video with a freelance editor. Spend on thumbnails and research before editing polish ([markstudios](https://www.markstudios.com/blog/faceless-youtube-video-cost-2026)).

## A 90-day plan from late November 2026 to February 2027

The plan below is my synthesis of the sources above, not a sourced recipe. It assumes one researched long-form video a week and rotates continents so the playlist structure is visible early. The topic ideas are my own illustrative suggestions of documented events, not taken from the research notes; check each one, and handle the violent ones carefully for ad suitability.

| Week | Main work | Upload | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (before launch) | Pick the channel name and documentary-style branding; write the About note on AI voice and human research; choose one premium voice; set up a source-log template; research and script videos 1–3; design 3 thumbnail/title options for each | None | Is each of the first 3 scripts fully sourced? |
| 1 | Publish video 1 (strongest, most famous story, e.g. a North American declassified program) plus 2 Shorts | 1 long + 2–3 Shorts | CTR, 30-second retention |
| 2 | Video 2 (Europe); keep researching 2 videos ahead | 1 + Shorts | Compare CTR with video 1 |
| 3 | Video 3 (South America) | 1 + Shorts | First retention dips: what to cut next time |
| 4 | Video 4 (Asia); review weeks 1–4 and drop the weakest title style | 1 + Shorts | Which continent and title style led? |
| 5 (late Dec) | Video 5 (Africa); keep the cadence through the holidays by finishing this one early | 1 + Shorts | Lower-tier progress (500 subs / 3,000 hours) |
| 6 | Video 6 (Australia/Oceania); create continent playlists | 1 + Shorts | Browse vs search traffic share |
| 7 | Video 7: a second take on the best-performing continent or theme | 1 + Shorts | Is any video above 5x channel average? |
| 8 | Video 8: try a new structure, such as an investigation-style or "three claims tested" episode | 1 + Shorts | Different structure, different retention? |
| 9 | Video 9: a longer cut (20–30 min) only if a previous video held retention well | 1 + Shorts | Average view duration vs length |
| 10 | Video 10: follow-up or sequel to your top video | 1 + Shorts | Returning viewers, subscriber conversion |
| 11 | Video 11; re-title and re-thumbnail any underperformer with good retention | 1 + Shorts | CTR change after repackaging |
| 12 | Video 12; audit the channel the way a YPP reviewer would (varied formats, sources in descriptions, no reused clips) | 1 + Shorts | Watch hours toward 8,000 |
| 13 (mid-Feb 2027) | Video 13; decide what to do next: double down on the winning continent or theme, consider moving to 2 a week only if research quality holds | 1 + Shorts | Honest scorecard against the odds below |

Throughout, reply to comments in the first hours after upload, because loyal early viewers can Hype your videos. Treat any factual correction from a viewer as a gift: pin it and fix the description.

## How likely each three-month outcome really is

These probabilities are my judgment. I based them on the vidIQ medians (46 long-form uploads and about 16 months to 1,000 subscribers), the absence of any verified fast-growth precedent in this niche, and the watch-hour arithmetic above. They assume a beginner following the plan with good research, about 13 long-form videos in total. A channel that uses unedited AI stories would do worse on every line, mainly through rejection or demotion risk.

| Outcome by about day 90 | Estimated probability | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| At least one video above 10K views | 25–40% | Small-channel outliers at 7–49x average show this is achievable with sharp packaging |
| 500 subscribers | 30–40% | Roughly a third of the way to the median 1K pace, helped by long-form documentary depth |
| 1,000 subscribers | 15–25% | About 13 uploads against a median of 46; needs one breakout video |
| Lower tier unlocked (500 subs + 3,000 hours) | 5–10% | 3,000 hours needs about 36,000 views at 5-minute average view duration |
| 10,000 subscribers | 3–5% | Needs a video in the hundreds of thousands of views; no verified niche precedent |
| Full ad monetization approved (1K subs + 8,000 hours) | 1–3% | Needs about 60,000–100,000 long-form views plus about a month of review |
| 100,000+ subscribers ("blow up") | under 1% | Only unverified or disputed claims exist; leaders took years |

The more useful question is what happens by **month 12**. If the format finds its audience, vendor timelines and the vidIQ data suggest **1,000 subscribers between months 6 and 18** is the realistic range, with 8,000 watch hours often arriving later. So plan to fund the channel for a year with no ad income.

## Conclusion

The real advantage in this plan is not speed or AI. It is that a carefully sourced, continent-by-continent series about documented conspiracies sits where the AI-slop crackdown is weakest and audience trust is strongest. The same evidence-first approach protects you from inauthentic-content rejection, borderline demotion and ad limits at once, and it is also what separates Fern and The Why Files from the terminated AI channels. The doubled 8,000-hour bar changes the economics of a fast launch: viral Shorts or a single hit no longer buys monetization, while a back catalogue of 15–20 minute documentaries that people actually finish does.

Three things remain unknown and are worth checking yourself: YouTube's exact Help-page wording for the 2026 policies and the February 2027 threshold, whether Narravox has a location feature and grants commercial rights, and whether non-Western conspiracy topics draw enough search demand (test with vidIQ or Google Trends before committing a whole season).
