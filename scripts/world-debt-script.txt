By the middle of 2025, the world owed roughly 340 trillion dollars. That's governments, companies and households added together, more than three times the size of the entire global economy. Most people assume this money is owed to someone powerful. China, maybe. Or a handful of billionaire bankers. And sure, China lends to dozens of countries. But here's the thing. In 2025, America owed more than 38 trillion dollars, and China held under 800 billion of it. About 2 percent. Back in 2013, China held more than 1.3 trillion. So if it isn't China, who is it?
If every country is in debt... who is the money actually owed to? It's a simple question with a complicated answer. Because when you follow the money, it never leaves the planet. It loops through banks, pension funds and central banks, and it ends up somewhere much closer to home than you'd expect. But that raises a second question, one we'll only answer at the very end. If the whole world is borrowing from itself... what happens on the day the lenders say no?
Start with something small. Two neighbours in a village. One has a spare bag of rice. The other needs rice now, and promises to return two bags after the harvest. That promise is a debt. But look at it from the other side. For the neighbour holding the promise, it's savings. One person's debt is always another person's asset. Every dollar owed is a dollar owned. Hold on to that idea, because it explains everything that follows.
Humans have been writing these promises down for nearly 4,000 years. Around 1750 BC, the Code of Hammurabi in Babylon set rules for interest on loans of grain and silver. In 1694, the Bank of England was founded to lend 1.2 million pounds to King William the Third for his war against France. In 1790, the brand-new United States took on roughly 75 million dollars of war debts, and paid the interest on time to prove it could be trusted. By 1815, after more than two decades of war against Napoleon, Britain's debt had climbed to roughly 200 percent of its economy. Yet it didn't collapse. Its lenders were mostly its own landowners, merchants and banks, and the interest stayed at home. In 1917, America sold Liberty Bonds to ordinary citizens to pay for the First World War, raising about 21 billion dollars. And on August 15, 1971, President Nixon cut the dollar's link to gold. From then on, government debt was backed by nothing but the promise to pay.
Now, that word "bond" matters. A bond is simply an IOU from a government or a company. You lend it money, it pays you interest every year, and it hands your money back on a set date. In 1917, a Liberty Bond could cost as little as 50 dollars, and a factory worker could pay for one in weekly instalments. That worker wasn't just funding a war. They had become the government's lender.
Fast forward a century. In 2024, the International Monetary Fund estimated that government debt worldwide would pass 100 trillion dollars for the first time, about 93 percent of everything the world produces in a year. Add companies and households, and the Institute of International Finance put the total at roughly 340 trillion by mid-2025.
Try to picture that. In 100 dollar bills, 340 trillion dollars is about 3.4 trillion notes. Each one is roughly a tenth of a millimetre thick. Stack them, and the pile rises past the clouds, past the International Space Station, and keeps climbing for about 370,000 kilometres, almost all the way to the Moon. It would weigh around 3.4 million tonnes. Split evenly, that's roughly 41,000 dollars of debt for each of the 8.2 billion people on Earth.
So if the world can't borrow from Mars, who is holding all those IOUs?
Let's follow the biggest pile on the planet: America's. In October 2025, US federal debt passed 38 trillion dollars. The government borrows whenever it spends more than it collects in taxes. That yearly gap is called the deficit, and in the 2025 fiscal year it was about 1.8 trillion dollars. Every dollar of that gap was filled by selling bonds to somebody. So, who?
Stop number one is the strangest. The government owes about 7 trillion dollars to itself. Funds like Social Security collected more in payroll taxes than they paid out for decades, and lent the surplus to the Treasury. Social Security alone held roughly 2.7 trillion dollars of these IOUs at the end of 2024.
Stop number two is the Federal Reserve, America's central bank. A central bank is the institution that creates a country's money and sets its interest rates. When the economy stumbles, it can buy government bonds with newly created money. Between early 2020 and 2022, the Fed's balance sheet more than doubled, from about 4.2 trillion dollars to nearly 9 trillion. By 2025, it still held roughly 4.2 trillion dollars of Treasury debt.
Stop number three is the one people worry about: foreigners. In 2025, foreign investors and governments held about 9 trillion dollars of US debt. Japan held the most, around 1.1 trillion. Britain came second at roughly 900 billion. China was third, with under 800 billion. Why do they buy? Mostly for foreign reserves. Those are the savings a country keeps in other currencies, so it can pay for imports and defend its own money in a crisis. And the dollar is still the world's favourite place to keep them.
Now here's the surprising part. Take the roughly 30 trillion dollars held outside the government, subtract the Fed and subtract foreigners, and you're left with about 17 trillion. That belongs to Americans. Banks. Pension funds. Insurance companies. Mutual funds. State governments. And individual savers.
Picture a nurse in Ohio with a retirement account. Part of her fund sits in Treasury bonds. Her emergency savings sit in a money market fund, and in 2023 and 2024, with Fed rates above 5 percent, that fund paid her roughly 5 percent a year. Where did that interest come from? From the US Treasury. In other words, from taxpayers like her.
And this is where it reaches you. If you have a pension, a retirement account, an insurance policy, or just money in a bank, there's a good chance part of it is lent to a government. Banks park deposits in government bonds because they're treated as the safest asset on Earth. American money market funds alone held more than 7 trillion dollars in 2025, much of it in short-term government debt. And in Britain, in September 2022, when bond prices crashed after a surprise budget, pension funds were forced into a fire sale. The Bank of England stepped in with up to 65 billion pounds of emergency bond buying to stop the spiral. Millions of people's retirement savings were sitting in the blast zone.
The uncomfortable truth is that you are probably both sides of the deal. As a taxpayer, you owe the debt. As a saver, you own it. In the 2024 fiscal year, US net interest payments reached about 880 billion dollars, more than the country spent on its military. That money flowed from taxpayers to savers. Very often, the same people.
So if most of this debt is owed to ourselves, why not just pay it off?
Here's the twist. Paying off a national debt almost never happens. The United States did it exactly once, in January 1835, under President Andrew Jackson. Within two years, the country fell into the Panic of 1837, one of its worst financial crises of the century. Historians still argue about how closely the two were connected, but the debt came back almost immediately.
Instead, governments do something called rolling over. When an old bond comes due, they sell a new one and use the cash to repay the first. The debt never really ends. It just changes hands. In 2015, Britain finally repaid a set of ancient bonds that still contained money borrowed during the South Sea Bubble of 1720, the Napoleonic Wars and the First World War. Some of that money had been rolled over for nearly 300 years.
In the early 2000s, Australia ran budget surpluses and seriously debated whether to stop selling government bonds altogether. After a review in 2002, it decided in 2003 to keep selling them anyway. Banks and pension funds needed somewhere safe to park money. Without government debt, savers have nowhere safe to go.
No country shows this better than Japan. In 1990, Japanese government debt was just over 60 percent of its economy. Then its stock and property bubble burst. The Nikkei index had peaked at nearly 39,000 points in December 1989, and over the next two decades it lost around 80 percent of its value. The government spent heavily, year after year, to keep the economy afloat. By 2024, gross debt had reached more than 1,300 trillion yen, roughly 235 percent of GDP, the highest of any major rich country.
So who did Japan borrow from? Mostly itself. By 2024, the Bank of Japan owned roughly half of all government bonds. Foreign investors held only a small slice, around one-tenth. The rest sat with Japanese banks, insurers and pension funds, including the world's largest pension fund, which manages around 250 trillion yen. And behind them stand Japanese households, holding about 2,200 trillion yen of financial assets, roughly half of it in cash and bank deposits.
Picture a retired factory worker in Osaka. For years, her bank deposit earned 0.001 percent interest. On a million yen, that's 10 yen a year, less than the price of a single sweet. Her savings were quietly funding the government, and earning almost nothing for it. Meanwhile, by 2025, a 5 kilo bag of rice had climbed above 4,000 yen in supermarkets, roughly double the year before.
And that's why it can't stop. From 2016 to March 2024, the Bank of Japan held interest rates below zero and bought bonds to keep borrowing cheap. If it steps back, rates rise. Already, servicing the debt eats about a quarter of the national budget, around 28 trillion yen in the 2025 plan. By 2025, the 10 year bond yield had climbed to its highest level since 2008, and in July 2024, the yen sank to about 161 per dollar, its weakest since 1986.
But here's the strangest part. For more than three decades, starting in 1991, Japan was also the world's largest creditor nation, owning more abroad than it owed abroad. At the end of 2024, its net foreign assets hit a record of about 533 trillion yen, and only then did Germany edge ahead. Japan owes itself. And the world owes Japan.
Japan survives because it borrows from its own people, in a currency it controls. So what happens to a country that can do neither?
Meet Sri Lanka. An island of about 22 million people. In July 2019, the World Bank promoted it to upper-middle-income status, with income per person of about 4,000 dollars a year. But a big share of its government debt was owed to foreigners, in dollars. International bondholders held the largest slice, around 12 billion. Governments like China, Japan and India were lenders too. China became the most controversial. In 2017, struggling with debt, Sri Lanka leased its Hambantota port to a Chinese state-owned company for 99 years, in a deal worth about 1.1 billion dollars. Critics called it a debt trap. Other researchers argued the story was more complicated, noting that Chinese loans were only around a tenth of Sri Lanka's foreign debt. Either way, the money was owed outside the country.
Then came the shocks. In April 2019, the Easter Sunday bombings scared away tourists. In late 2019, the new government cut the main sales tax from 15 percent to 8 percent. The IMF later said the cuts badly weakened revenue. By 2020, credit agencies had downgraded the country, shutting it out of global markets. COVID froze tourism. And in April 2021, a sudden ban on chemical fertiliser, reversed that November, was widely blamed for shrinking harvests.
Sri Lanka needed dollars to pay its lenders. And it couldn't print them. Its foreign reserves fell from about 7.6 billion dollars at the end of 2019 to under 2 billion by March 2022. On April 12, 2022, it announced it would stop paying about 51 billion dollars of foreign debt. That's a default: when a borrower fails to pay what it owes, on time. It was the country's first default since independence in 1948.
For a teacher in Colombo, the numbers became daily life. The rupee fell from about 200 per dollar to around 360 within about two months. Petrol went from about 177 rupees a litre at the start of 2022 to 470 by that June. Drivers queued for days. Schools closed to save fuel. Inflation hit about 70 percent in September 2022, and food prices rose even faster. The World Bank estimated poverty roughly doubled, to about 1 in 4 people. Mass protests filled Colombo, and in July 2022, President Gotabaya Rajapaksa left the country and resigned.
In March 2023, the IMF approved a 2.9 billion dollar bailout. An IMF bailout is an emergency loan, given in exchange for tough reforms. Taxes rose, with sales tax reaching 18 percent in 2024. Electricity prices jumped. By late 2024, bondholders agreed to take a cut on what they were owed, and the economy grew again.
Notice the difference. Sri Lanka broke with public debt of roughly 128 percent of GDP in 2022. Japan's ratio is close to double that, and it still borrows cheaply. The danger was never just how much a country owes. It's who it owes, and in whose currency. Which brings us back to the question we started with. What happens when the lenders say no?
The answer depends on who you are. If you borrow from your own people, in your own currency, your central bank can step in, like the Bank of England did in 2022. The price isn't default. It's inflation and a weaker currency, quietly paid by savers. If you borrow from foreigners, in someone else's money, nobody can rescue you except the IMF.
From here, three futures are realistic. One, the slow squeeze. US interest costs passed roughly a trillion dollars in 2025, competing with roads, schools and defence. Two, inflation does the work. After the Second World War, US debt held by the public was about 106 percent of GDP in 1946. By 1974, it had fallen to around 23 percent, worn down by growth and rising prices. Savers paid for that, slowly. Three, the sudden stop. In 2023, developing countries paid a record 1.4 trillion dollars to service foreign debt, and the next Sri Lanka could be among them.
Now remember that stack of bills reaching almost to the Moon. 340 trillion dollars. It isn't owed to aliens, and it isn't mostly owed to China. It's owed to pension funds, banks, insurers and savers. Every debt has two sides. Governments sign the promise. Ordinary people hold it. The world isn't in debt to someone else. It's in debt to itself.
The lender is you.
